Abstract

We describe the design, measurement discipline, and initial results of a competitive Pokémon battle AI built for deployment on a production Minecraft server network, where the controlling constraint is not maximum strength but strength per millisecond: the agent must make decisions in single-digit milliseconds on CPU threads shared with 500+ concurrent players. We report two phases. Phase 1 built a classical system — Monte-Carlo tree search over a Rust battle engine with a calibrated linear evaluation and usage-prior policy — that reached ≈20% win rate against Foul Play (a strong open search engine, ≈1800–1930 Elo) at 100 ms/decision, then plateaued: preregistered experiments showed the gains from additional labels (+0.6pp from 1.1M teacher decisions), additional search budget (≈+1–2pp per 8× time), and additional feature engineering (≈+2pp per wave) had all collapsed, while a single controlled change of function class — replacing the linear policy prior with a small set-conditional network on identical inputs and labels — was worth +7.7pp of teacher agreement. We interpreted this as a capacity-and-experience limit rather than a tuning shortfall. Phase 2 rebuilt the system around large-scale self-play reinforcement learning in the style of Jaxcalibur: a vendored Rust simulator verified against the official Pokémon Showdown implementation by differential testing (turn agreement raised 31.1% → 87.4% across a documented ledger of 25 landed patches over 28 numbered, the most recent wave measured and deployed into a running training fleet), a masked 60-token observation with a machine-checked no-information-leakage property, and PPO self-play training a 2.5–8.1M-parameter transformer at 4,500–5,600 decisions/second on one H100. A transfer harness plays training checkpoints against Foul Play in real Showdown battles through the same encoder code path used in training, with a cross-path golden test (3.37M values, one explained divergence channel) and a public ledger of approximations.

Two 24-hour runs — 8.1M and 2.49M parameters, identical environment and budget, 12.17M self-play games between them — took net-only transfer win rate against full-strength Foul Play from a pooled untrained floor of 0.1% (1/700) to a late-run pooled 27.2% [24.5, 30.0] and 27.3% [24.6, 30.1] respectively, with best single arms of 34% and 36%. The within-run rise is unambiguous (first third of the treadmill 18.5% → last third 27.8%, +9.3pp, p < 0.0001), and it exceeds the Phase-1 search system's career ≈20% at 1/12 the decision latency and with no search. The size ablation reads null on the matched comparisons — whole-run pooled (p = 0.54), late-run pooled (p = 0.96), peaks (p = 0.77), and best-against-best over eight 500-game soaks (p = 0.57) — so the quarter-size net, which generates 22% more experience per GPU-hour co-located and ≈1.8× more per dollar in the actor-bound regime, is the configuration we scaled. That scaled run is now closed. Launched 2026-08-31, warm-started from a soak-certified 27.4% checkpoint and training for the first time against the corrected simulator, it was paused by decision at iteration 6,633 after 7.56M self-play games — because its curve stopped moving, not because anything broke. Measured over 83 deep-evaluation arms and 24,857 games against full-strength Foul Play, it pooled to 38.54% [37.93, 39.14] whole-run, rose to a best band of 42.4% [40.7, 44.2], and peaked at 47.7% [42.1, 53.3] on a two-way tie between checkpoints 4328 and 4984. Its final third is flat on three independent cuts of the data (−1.1 to −1.6pp, p = 0.23–0.30) while the same instrument resolved the earlier rise at p = 0.0002 — so this is a stopped curve, not an underpowered ruler. We decline to call it a capacity ceiling: the run carried no matched larger-network arm and therefore contains no capacity comparison at all, and §8.12 sets out why the tempting inference is unsupported by anything we measured. Two evaluation rigs still disagree by a well-characterised +2.6pp systematic offset (55 matched arms, paired p ≈ 0.0005), cause unidentified.

Three results in this revision are negative, corrective, or pre-registered, and are reported as such. A second differential-testing wave took turn agreement 85.934% → 87.372% on a re-established corpus (+1.438pp, 980 battles / 65,321 turns) and was carried from analysis to a live cut into the running fleet in one day — but its adversarial verification pass found that one of its four patches was still inert in continuous play, and that the conformance harness could not have seen this, because the harness re-supplies every turn the state the defect corrupts (§8.9). Wrapping the trained network in a determinized PUCT search at its own 100 ms serving budget moved the win rate by −1.4pp against the same checkpoint and opponent — a multiplier of zero, with the mechanism measured rather than guessed (CPU inference buys ≈37 simulations per decision; the search agreed with the greedy policy on 97.3% of them), and an unmatched 18× budget made the search act on 13.9% of decisions while still not clearing the anchor's interval (§8.10). And a finding this paper previously carried as one of three named plateau mechanisms — that the agent switches too rarely — does not replicate and is withdrawn (§8.5): measured on the same denominator on both sides, we switch 2.6× more than the opponent, not less.

This revision adds four more of the same kind, two of them expensive to have learned any other way. The pre-registered physics cut of §8.11 returned the outcome we had labelled most informative: no dip and no separable step, with a +2.3 to +3.0pp effect whose p-value crosses 0.05 as the comparison window widens — reported as a full sensitivity table, together with the admission that pre-registering an endpoint without its window is not full pre-registration. A value-objective change aimed squarely at the paper's headline defect returned "no verdict, in either direction" after the analysis was corrected for battle-level clustering: the same buckets read p = 0.0017 and p = 0.018 treating decisions as independent and p = 0.57 and p = 0.39 once five decisions per battle stop counting as five observations (§8.13). And a $3.17 probe of a rented H100 found that at every batch size an actor fleet would use it is slower than the $0.41/hour consumer card we already own — 0.75–0.94× — and 4.2× worse per unit of work, because wall time per step is flat from batch 1 to batch 16,000 on both cards and a cost that does not move with batch size is host-side dispatch, not silicon. The port is 31.0M battle-turns/second on the consumer card (481× a single-threaded numpy baseline); the fix that follows from the diagnosis removes 332 of its 404 GPU dispatches per step, is worth 1.7–3.3× at actor-realistic batches, and moves the scanned kernel to device-bound from B ≈ 4,096 rather than B ≈ 16,000–65,536 (§8.15). The fourth negative is the crossover gauntlet (§10.2): the campaign's rule for replacing the shipped Rust fork is that sim2 must beat it on the fork's own ground, and on a shared 984-battle corpus it scores 62.140% against 87.372% — it does not cross over, and the engine is not replaced. That verdict survived a correction that moved the headline +4.19pp, because the gauntlet's first run was scored through a loader that resolved 100% of weather turns to "no weather" (§7). Separately, a doubles simulator was built in one day by seven concurrent agent lanes and graded from its first commit, taking roll-conditioned turn agreement from 26.03% to 50.00% and its HP error from 12,501 to 5,060 as the last lane merged — and its verification pass found four residual effects being applied twice per turn because two lanes had registered the same mechanic under different names, a collision that disjoint file ownership does not prevent and no single lane's tests could see (§8.14).

This revision closes the campaign's 8M line and adds a measurement finding that qualifies every strength number above. A distilled 8.13M student reproduced its 2.5M teacher's strength — teacher agreement 0.848, 44.3% [38.8, 50.0] against full-strength Foul Play — and was banked as the artifact of record; three reinforcement-learning runs on top of it then destroyed that strength, and two explanations (an entropy bonus, and a "learning-rate experiment" that had in fact moved the rate the wrong way) were falsified before the cause was found. The cause was a learning rate inherited unchanged from a network a third the width: at 7e-5 rather than 3e-4 the trust region returns inside the band a healthy 6,600-iteration run defines (clip_frac 0.188, approx_kl 0.0265) and the entropy wash slows 5.5×, to 0.00032 per iteration. The recipe's pre-registered strength gate nonetheless failed — iteration 260 scores 39.7% against its own window's 44.0% seed anchor, with the slope positive at +4.4pp and the level short — over a run of roughly 300,000 self-play games. We report that as the finding it is: the recipe is stable and not sample-efficient at CPU game-generation rates, which is the strongest evidence this campaign has produced for GPU-scale generation and is a negative result rather than a benchmark. The measurement finding is that an opponent with a compute budget is not a fixed bar: the same checkpoint against the same opponent over the same 300 games scores 44.3% on a quiet box and 54.0% on a loaded one (z = 2.37, p = 0.018; forward-pass p50 18.8 → 73.5 ms), because Foul Play is time-budgeted and background load takes its search allowance away. Fixed percentage bars are therefore invalid on shared rigs — the gate was rewritten to compare each arm against a reference checkpoint shipped into its own load window — and the general rule, which applies to any benchmark whose reference player searches on a clock, is that the opponent's compute must be controlled. Separately, the doubles-first simulator's singles agreement moved 70.4455% → 72.432% in one day against 17 verifier-caught defects, and a re-diagnosis re-ranked its remaining backlog by activated exposure rather than by holder presence, deflating the largest filed candidate 5,311 → 58 and relocating a third of the residual gap into the turns where the oracle logged that nothing happened (§8.19).

The most recent revision reports a failure to reproduce our own headline and the platform built to explain it. Three interventions cut into the live learner — an entropy controller, a loss-rebalance annealer, and the trunk released as both auxiliary heads reached minimum scale — were scored against the baseline written for exactly that purpose, and the re-run reads 50.54% [50.05, 51.03] against 52.12% [51.64, 52.61]: disjoint intervals, −1.58pp, ≈ −11 Elo, with the tool's null runs reproducing the baseline's run for run. Training loss fell 0.7142 → 0.2864 over the same window, which is the objective shrinking, not the policy improving — the annealer floors the auxiliary coefficients while value_loss moves 0.5112 → 0.5142, worse, and the opponent-action head sits at chance (0.5019). The cuts are held pending a re-measure at a matched iteration count. Against the external opponent under constant conditions the picture matches: 8.1M scores 40.17% [37.53, 42.86] (n = 1,297) against a matched 2.49M control's 41.64%, p = 0.34, and an earlier +2.96pp is retracted to +1.10pp (p = 0.39). Two independent failures to find a capacity effect, and a live intervention wave with a negative net effect, convert this campaign's argument from "not sample-efficient" into a starvation thesis — which the rest of the revision is the machinery for. The mechanics port was pre-graded at the union of its five handler lanes rather than lane by lane, moving GPU-side agreement 18.48% → 87.98% on singles and 20.55% → 68.49% on doubles (97.17% / 95.21% as pure transcription), with a union gain more than twice the sum of its lanes' because coverage composes multiplicatively. The observation encoder moved onto the device for 44–120× on encoding and 52.9 → 97.4 battles/s end to end at 2,048 environments on one consumer card (GPU utilisation 43% → 70%), verified ids- and mask-bit-exact with floats inside a derived 1-ULP bound over 12,007 real states. sim2's own accuracy stack took its largest single-lane step of the campaign, +0.8694pp (566 turns fixed against 1 broken, p = 5.05 × 10⁻¹²⁴), re-derived independently to sixteen digits. And the GPU training lineage launched: a fresh 8.19M-parameter, 89-wide, entropy-controlled run, quarantined beside the CPU fleet rather than cut over onto it, because the cutover was measured impossible by construction (the learner refuses shards across the encoding seam) and undesirable in fact (the GPU actor was 35% slower than a CPU actor on the same pod, at 0% GPU utilisation, before the encoder was fixed). It is a full-information run until reveal masks are built on the JAX side, and no win rate it produces is comparable to any other number in this paper until they are.

The most recent revision closes the CPU era on its own evidence and opens the next one on measured hardware. The held cuts were re-measured at a matched iteration count and the dip reversed in full — 52.31% [51.82, 52.80] over 40,000 games, +1.77pp and disjoint above the 50.54% (p = 5.6 × 10⁻⁷) — but the number that mattered is the one against the pre-cut baseline: +0.18pp [−0.51, +0.87], p = 0.61, so 2,050 iterations of intervention bought nothing, with the value head moving 0.004 across the whole span. Grading the run's entire life against the external opponent gives the same answer from a second instrument: 83 arms across 5,574 iterations are flat noise (mean ≈42.5%, range 35.7–52.0% at n = 300). The era was retired by decision, its boxes archived md5-verified and destroyed, and the 8M frozen as an immutable anchor. The successor ran its first day on four H100s, where the finding is that the learner is the wall: one process on one card at 3,030 samples/s against a single actor's 8,340, 66.4% of an iteration spent reading shards — a fifth of it a checksum — and 62% of generation discarded stale. Two rungs took it to 4,884 samples/s (+61%), both reported with their claims corrected: a minibatch change A/B'd at +43% settled at +20% because the probe's feed shape differed from the lineage's, and turning the checksum off over-delivered at +35.7%. The actors measured ≈17 battles/s per card at 1% GPU utilisation, host-bound, which discharges this paper's own ≈150–250 battles/s projection with an order-of-magnitude miss. Against that, the round's positive result is the campaign's first transferable strength measurement: an 89-wide seat that plays real Showdown battles against the pinned anchor under real hidden information, built as five new files touching not one line of any existing one, producing a monotone ladder — 0.00% [0.00, 3.70] untrained → 4.00% → 9.25% [6.79, 12.49] at iteration 249 — whose floor does not overlap its head, which is what licenses reading the level as the lineage's rather than the instrument's. The reveal mask that number is waiting on is built and verified (144 rows × two arms, zero mismatches, zero excused cells; twelve mutants caught; a thirteenth published as a finding) and deliberately not yet wired in. And the round's theme is its five corrections printed beside their originals, of which the sharpest is that a 170–250× speedup this paper published was a caching artefact — a device array caches its host value, so the benchmark timed a dictionary lookup — with the real figure 10.8–17.1× on the segment and 1.54–1.75× on the step. §7 gains an eighth verification class for the case these share: the code is right, the verifier is right, and the reading carried out of them is not.

The current revision reports a night in which the campaign deleted its own best result on purpose. The GPU lineage had trained on full information since launch under a disclosed banner; the owner ruled the data poison, and it was stopped, archived and replaced by a masked lineage that starts from zero. What went with it was the highest number this campaign had recorded against its pinned anchor — 18.67% [13.24, 25.66] over 150 real Showdown games, up from 7.33% in one evening — withdrawn not because it was measured wrong but because the network that produced it read the opponent's exact stats, item, moves and HP. Masking turned out to be free (4,858 samples/s against 4,921), and two masked lineages ignited three and a half hours apart because pre-ignition verification kept finding defects in the model's inputs while a restart was still cheap. The substance of the round is that verification ledger: five defects, none visible to a green build, each caught by a different layer of the protocol — a device encoder that could not be constructed against anything but a test stub; a single omitted argument whose default was full information, so a run labelled masked would have cheated; a distributed learner whose heartbeat, written by truncating it in place, let a peer sweep a live rank (233 of 530 reads) and in the worst case train the same shard twice; three of four history channels carrying the opponent's exact team HP including off-field healing no player can see; and a fix for the last of these that reached one of the two encoder paths. Two of the five were caught by fail-closed launch gates on the first real construction of the encoder, which is §7's ninth verification class and its corollary: a gate is evidence only once it has been shown to fail. The audit's negative results are reported beside the positive ones — training targets and the 89-wide action space verified clean by execution — and two limits were deliberately frozen rather than fixed, at the one moment fixing them would have been free. The round therefore ends with no strength number at all, which is the honest position and a worse-looking one than the position it replaced.

A secondary contribution is methodological: the entire system — engine patches, trainers, harnesses, and the experiments themselves — was written by AI coding agents under human direction, with correctness maintained not by human code review but by an adversarial verification protocol described in §7. We report the defects this protocol caught, including three that would have silently invalidated headline results, and a two-probe verification campaign (§8.7) that spent $4.11 on rented hardware to find 27 flaws in a stack whose test suites were entirely green — among them a trainer that never published its weights, which rendered an earlier probe's learning curve uninterpretable.

1Introduction

1.1 The product constraint

Cobblemon Eclipse operates Minecraft servers where players battle AI-controlled trainers using Cobblemon, a faithful implementation of Pokémon battle mechanics. The design target is 500+ concurrent players on shared region-worker threads. This inverts the usual competitive-AI budget: Foul Play, the strongest open Pokémon engine, spends ≈100 ms of a dedicated core per decision — one battle per core. At 500 concurrent battles that is a non-starter. Every architectural decision in this project traces to the question: maximum strength per millisecond on a shared-thread budget, never "maximum strength."

1.2 The benchmark ladder

Strength is measured against a ladder of opponents, each adopted when the previous one stopped predicting real-world performance: a baseline open bot (Abyssal; beaten 79.4%), an LLM-agent system (PokeChamp; beaten 14–5 pooled), human ladder players (Phase 1: 1W–15L on natural-completion endings, the strict convention this paper quotes throughout; 7W–15L raw, the difference being four opponent forfeits inside ten turns — and see §8.16 for the first post-pivot human games), and finally Foul Play itself, whose defeat of skilled humans makes it the standing altimeter: ≈17% ≈ 280 Elo below FP; ≈30% ≈ strong ladder play; ≈50% ≈ competitive with the best humans. All results in this paper are against full-strength Foul Play; results against time-reduced FP are used only for triage and never reported as progress (a standing "grade-inflation guard," adopted after a time-starved win of +4pp failed to survive full strength).

1.3 Two phases

Phase 1 (§3–4) is a classical search system, developed through nine audited improvement rounds. Phase 2 (§5–6) is a from-scratch self-play RL system, adopted after preregistered experiments located the Phase-1 plateau in representation and experience volume rather than in any tunable parameter. §7 describes the verification protocol that substituted for human review. §8 reports results. §9 states limitations, and §10 points at the road to doubles, National Dex, serving, and a co-evolving team builder.

2Related Work

  • Foul Play — expectiminimax-style search over poke-engine with hand-tuned evaluation; the strongest open traditional engine and this project's reference opponent.
  • PokeChamp / LLM agents — minimax over LLM-proposed actions; strong but orders of magnitude too slow and too expensive per decision for our deployment target.
  • Jaxcalibur (2026) — the first bot at top-human level in gen9 random battles (2557 Elo, #1 ladder): a pure-JAX simulator, PPO self-play at ≈100M games/week on one H100, an 8.5M-parameter transformer with matchup tokens and an opponent-action auxiliary head, and the striking finding that inference-time search adds only +100–150 Elo on top of the trained network's intuition.   • AlphaZero and successors — establish the self-play paradigm; we follow the now-standard observation that a policy trained by self-play carries most of the strength, with search as a multiplier rather than the substrate.

3Phase 1: The Search System

A summary; the full engineering record is the project's intelligence audit (775 numbered findings at time of writing).

  • Engine. A vendored fork of poke-engine (Rust), extended with an "eclipse fidelity" option module (§5.2) and a wire protocol to the JVM game server.
  • Search. MCTS with determinized hidden information (K sampled "worlds"), a calibrated linear-logistic leaf evaluator (hazards, boosts, KO and HP terms; AUC 0.834, calibration verified against outcomes), and a usage-frequency policy prior; ≈100 ms budget, ≈50k iterations/decision.
  • Result. ≈20% vs full-strength Foul Play at pivot time; deployed behind config gates as the server's strongest available brain.

4Why We Pivoted: Locating the Plateau

Four preregistered findings, in sequence:

  1. The budget curve was flat. 100 → 800 ms of search bought ≈+1–2pp. Iterations were not the binding constraint (zero deadline hits; win rate flat in iterations).
  2. Labels were not the constraint. Distilling 1.11M Foul Play teacher decisions into the shipped linear prior moved teacher agreement +0.56pp (bar: +10pp; P(clears)=0), and a human-refit control attributed approximately none of it to the corpus. The prior was representation-limited, not label-limited.
  3. Capacity was the constraint. Holding labels and the 41 input columns fixed and changing only the function class — a set-conditional network scoring each candidate in the context of the others — was worth +7.14pp top-1 teacher agreement (+7.70pp paired, [+7.24, +8.16], on 129,428 held-out decisions). The 2×2 ablation showed the gain requires capacity and set context jointly; either alone read null.
  4. Offline agreement is a screening metric. Routed through the search as a root prior, the +7.7pp converted to ≤+4pp of win rate (best estimate +1.8). We adopted a standing rule: preregister behavioural endpoints (paired win rate against a real opponent); offline agreement only screens.

Combined with Jaxcalibur's public result, the conclusion was that strength lives in (capacity × experience volume), and our 3,000-game corpora were four orders of magnitude short. The pivot decision was made 2026-08-30.

5Phase 2: The Self-Play System

5.1 Architecture

A 60-token observation per decision: 1 field token, 2 active tokens, 5 candidate-move tokens, 6 + 6 team tokens, 16 recent-event history tokens, 24 matchup tokens (per-pair damage estimates, injected mid-network). Eleven categorical id slots (species, item, ability, move, types, tera, status) over vocabularies extracted from the engine's own enums with a committed-cache cross-check; 269 float channels. The network is a pre-norm transformer (256/1024/8, 8.1M parameters, and a 128/512/8, 2.49M variant — the two arms of §8.2) with four heads: policy (14 actions), value, opponent-next-action (auxiliary), and opponent-set auxiliaries. Training is PPO, reward 1/0 undiscounted, entropy and KL-to-uniform regularisation, following the Jaxcalibur recipe.

5.2 The simulator, and how we know it tells the truth

The training environment is the vendored Rust engine — the same one Phase 1 searched over — which raises the central question of any home-grown simulator: is the physics right? Our answer is continuous differential testing against the official Pokémon Showdown implementation, pinned at a fixed commit as the oracle:

  • A Showdown-side dumper emits per-turn state fixtures from real random battles; a comparator drives the Rust engine through the same battles and classifies every divergence by mechanic into a registry of named classes, each carrying a status (OPEN / APPROX / UNSUPPORTED) and a size.
  • Engine corrections are made as a numbered, hash-guarded patch ledger over the vendored source (28 numbered, 25 landed at time of writing), each patch either unconditional (pure correctness) or gated behind a fidelity option when upstream's own tests pin the old behaviour.
  • Headline through patch 21: turn agreement 31.1% (shipped configuration) → 68.2% → 79.6% (17 patches) → 85.73% [85.46, 86.00] with patches 18–21 and fidelity options on (986 battles, 66,034 turns, measured 2026-08-30). Reward-field agreement (win/loss/HP outcomes) rose to 92.09%. The PP-decrement class fell 86% (2,187 → 306 divergent turns), the damage class −2.89pp, and the Curse class closed outright.
  • Wave 2 (§8.9) takes it to 87.372%, on a corpus that had to be re-established because the 986-battle one behind 85.73% was generated on a rented pod that no longer exists. On the surviving 980-battle corpus (65,321 turns, same pinned oracle) the pre-wave engine scores 85.934% and the post-wave engine 87.372%, +1.438pp; the unclassified bucket falls 3,117 → 2,155 turns. The canonical conformance reference from here forward is the committed report harness/selfplay/reports/CONFORMANCE_2026-09-01-wave2.md, because it is the only one whose corpus anyone can still open. 85.934% and 85.733% are two draws of the same generator against adjacent points on the same tree, not the same number measured twice, and we state the discontinuity rather than splice the series.
  • The ranked residual driving the next wave, after wave 2: damage-roll residue after outcome-forcing 6.13% (of which ≈93% is genuine divergence and only ≈7% attainable) is now clearly the largest item; the unclassified bucket has fallen from 4.91% to 3.30% and is no longer the leader; VOLATILE_UNMODELLED 1.19% (the lockedmove volatile is its top entry, and §8.9 records why the obvious fix is the wrong one); PP 0.53%; boost divergence 0.364%; and the two classes still marked OPEN, Toxic-counter-on-switch and Trace, at 0.106% and 0.060%.
  • What a conformance number is not: this gauntlet enumerates every branch the engine could have taken and asks whether one of them reproduces what Showdown did (a mean of ≈57 branches per stepped turn). The training environment deliberately samples one roll per turn, because a policy must act under the same uncertainty a real opponent faces. "Can the engine's branch set explain what Showdown did" and "what does one played-out game look like" are different questions, and 87.372% answers only the first.
  • Notable finding for practitioners: two of the five originally-open divergence classes were defects in the measurement harness, not the engine — a fainted-Pokémon projection and a Tera-typing schema gap. A conformance number is only as good as its comparator, and the comparator needs its own tests.
  • A second methodological rule came out of the re-measurement. The Curse class had been sized at 1.29% from a five-sample forensic dump; at deep sampling it had zero occurrences — the original figure was an artifact of sizing a class from the same handful of samples used to diagnose its mechanism. The rule we now hold: a small forensic dump may diagnose a mechanism or size a class, never both.

5.3 Hidden information, machine-checked

Pokémon is an imperfect-information game; a self-play agent that trains on information a real opponent would hide posts beautiful curves and cannot transfer. The environment therefore carries a reveal tracker (what each seat has legitimately observed) publishing eleven mask arrays, and the encoder consumes only the masked view. The property is enforced by tests, not convention: two battles differing in exactly one hidden fact must produce byte-identical viewer tokens and differing owner tokens over randomized populations (Python and Rust twins of the same argument), and the leakage suite is a CI gate wired to the exact files a leak would be written in. Three real leaks were found and closed this way during construction (bench-HP, a declared-but-dead move, and a team-HP channel bypassing the mask).

5.4 Throughput and cost

The full-fidelity environment (masked observation, real random-battle team pool pinned to a set-table commit, sampled damage rolls) runs at ≈327k transitions/second on the sims box — 2.2× cheaper than enumerating all 16 damage rolls per branch would be at equal fidelity (a 586× difference on that axis alone) and 3.3× above the training contract's floor. On one H100 SXM the full loop (1,024 parallel battles, batched inference for both seats, PPO update) sustained a run-mean of 4,551 samples/second at 8.1M parameters and 5,551 at 2.49M — about 915 completed battles per training iteration of 65,536 samples, i.e. 238k and 289k games per GPU-hour. Day-one cost: $172 for the two 24-hour runs, or $14.13 per million self-play games. §8.3 reports what happens to that figure once the loop is taken off a rented H100 and onto hardware chosen for the resource it is actually bound by — and what the real bottleneck turned out to be once it got there.

6Measuring Transfer Honestly

6.1 The adapter

Self-play curves cannot certify strength (a net can master its simulator's quirks). The transfer harness plays training checkpoints in real Showdown battles via poke-env, against Foul Play at full strength, with the network thinking for ≈8 ms on CPU and no search. The design rule: the adapter builds the environment-contract buffers and then calls the same code objects the trainer uses (encoder, matchup, event derivation) — the only place the training and serving paths can diverge is one translation file, which is exactly where the testing effort concentrates.

A second adapter now exists for the GPU lineage, and the design rule was tightened rather than copied. The GPU networks speak an 89-wide action space against the CPU harness's 14-wide one, so they need their own seat. It was built as five new files that change not one line of any existing one — because the shared files it reuses are precisely the files whose tests certify the training/serving seam, so editing one would be editing the thing those tests certify. Its widening step is not a re-parameterisation: the 14-wide image had no column at all for Struggle or Recharge, so both landed in move slot 0 and the harness sent correct wire bytes for the wrong reason. §8.30 reports the lift, the two indexing traps it caught in the choice renderer, and the first strength ladder it produced.

6.2 The golden, and the approximations ledger

A cross-path golden encodes 200 generated positions through both paths and compares 3,372,600 values; at time of writing the tolerated divergence is 70 values (0.0021%), all on one explained matchup channel, pinned by an assertion that fails if the count drifts or a new divergence class appears (mutation-tested). Everything the adapter cannot know or chooses to mirror from training is a numbered approximation (A1–A13) in a public ledger, each with its expected cost and a measurement plan; the two capable of moving results (opponent slot compaction; inferred opponent actions) are counted in every series report.

A9 is now closed. The largest of these — inferred opponent actions, unobserved on ≈55% of decisions — was resolved by reading Showdown's |move| protocol line directly instead of inferring from a PP rule the measurement fork never exercised. Measured after the fix: the unknown-action rate fell 56.4% → 19.8% (2.8×), the old heuristics fired zero times, and there were zero fallbacks. The residual was then decomposed and found benign: 469 of the 539 remaining windows are decisions on which the opponent genuinely took no action (switch-ins after a faint, forced turns), and the other 70 are the "can't act" floor; a line-level reconstruction put the real-gap component at zero, both by measurement and by construction.

6.3 The treadmill

During training, a loop picks up each mirrored checkpoint (≈30-minute cadence) and plays a 100-game series against full-strength Foul Play, plus a 50-game untrained-control series as the floor, writing to the same dashboard as every other experiment. The protocol is two-tier by design: hourly 100-game series read the slope (±≈8–9pp at n = 100), and a 300–500-game soak on a rented CPU pod certifies any number that must count. Measurement lags the live network by ≈100 iterations (≈45 minutes), so the plotted curve systematically understates the current net.

A 100-game arm is too noisy to carry a claim on its own, and we do not let one. Where §8 reports a level rather than a slope, it pools consecutive arms into a band and quotes the Wilson interval on the pooled denominator — eleven arms is 1,100 games, which is soak-grade. Pooling consecutive checkpoints of a still-improving policy trades a small downward bias (the band averages over the improvement inside it) for a large variance reduction; we accept that trade and state the bias rather than quoting a peak arm. Peak arms are reported as peaks, never as the run's level — an obvious rule that is easy to break once a 36% appears on a dashboard.

The treadmill's reference opponent is being demoted, and the reason is information per game. Every level in §8.4 through §8.19 was read against full-strength Foul Play, which the ladder of §1.2 adopted precisely because it predicts human performance. Three properties have since made it a poor instrument for the day-to-day question of whether an intervention worked. It is time-budgeted, so its strength is a function of the host's load and not only of ours (§8.18, a term this paper has to carry on every historical number). It is far from us, so a 100-game arm at ≈40% resolves far less than the same 100 games at ≈50% would. And it is slow and external, which puts a price on every point of resolution.

From §8.21 onward the primary validation instrument is instead a pinned own-checkpoint anchor chosen to sit near a 50% win rate — a frozen network of ours, identified by md5, played role-averaged in our own environment. Near 50% each game carries the most information it can, the opponent's compute is ours and is constant, and the comparison is paired by construction. Foul Play is retained but demoted to a sparse milestone gate: the thing that says whether the campaign is going anywhere at all, run at a low cadence and at soak sample sizes, rather than the thing that scores each intervention. The trade is stated because it is a real one — an own-checkpoint anchor cannot detect that the whole lineage is drifting somewhere clever and useless, which is exactly the failure §6.1 exists to catch, and only the external opponent can. Keeping both, at different cadences and for different questions, is the design; §8.22 and §8.23 are the first pair of readings taken under it.

As of §8.28 the anchor is frozen and the treadmill has a third tier. The CPU lineage that supplied the anchor is retired, so it can no longer drift: it is an immutable opponent identified by md5, which removes the one thing an own-checkpoint anchor is most at risk of — a reference that quietly moves with the fleet it came from. Beside the in-simulator anchor there is now a standing Showdown-arena loop: every newly published GPU checkpoint plays a mirrored series against that same pinned anchor on a real Showdown server, under real hidden information, on a 15-minute cadence with a backfill queue for versions published before the loop existed. That gives the campaign three tiers rather than two — a fast in-simulator anchor for slope, a game-accurate anchor for transfer, and a sparse external-opponent gate for absolute level — and it is the first of the three that can see the failure §8.27's banner names, because a full-information network's loss between the two arenas is the quantity being asked about. Every row the loop writes carries the full-information disclosure verbatim in its own record, so the caveat travels with the data instead of living in a document someone has to remember to read.

6.4 Withdrawn numbers

Two early results were withdrawn by our own verification before anyone believed them, and we report them as method: (i) the first series (5%) was measured through an adapter that dropped ≈110 "special" moves (Protect, Knock Off, Recover, hazards, most status) from the agent's own moveset — poke-env's Pokemon.moves never stores them; only 183 of 871 move requests reached the net with all four slots, and all-special movesets produced illegal-mask fallbacks. (ii) a conformance report's headline was measured with a driver built before one of the five fixes it reported. Both defects were caught by adversarial re-derivation (§7), both fixes carry regression tests, and the corrected numbers replaced the originals within hours.

A third has since joined them, and it is the most instructive because nothing was broken when it was produced: (iii) the switch-stinginess finding of §8.5 — an aggregate behavioural rate compared across two systems — was arithmetically correct, semantically wrong, and survived three revisions of this paper because no test catches a number that is computed correctly on mismatched denominators. It is withdrawn in §8.5 with the better measurement printed beside it.

A fourth is different in kind from all three, because nothing about it was wrong. (iv) Every strength number the full-information GPU lineage produced is retired — the 0.00% / 4.00% / 9.25% ladder of §8.30, its 7.33% first point on the four-card run, and that run's best and final reading, 18.67% [13.24, 25.66] over 150 games against the pinned anchor on a real Showdown server, which was the highest figure this campaign had ever recorded against it. The instrument is the one §8.30 built and validated, the games were real, and the arithmetic re-derives. What is wrong is the subject: a network trained on the opponent's exact stats, item, moves and HP is not the network those numbers appear to describe, and a curve nobody may cite is a curve that will eventually be cited. The owner's ruling was to wipe them from the live graph and archive them aside rather than to keep them under a footnote (§8.33), and this paper follows the ruling. The methods result underneath §8.30 survives the retirement and is stated separately: a monotone ladder whose floor does not overlap its head is evidence about the seat — that it transmits skill — and that evidence does not depend on what the lineage behind it was trained on. The levels are gone; the instrument stands, and it now grades a masked lineage from a measured zero.

7Verification Without Human Review

This system was built by AI coding agents; no human reads the diffs. The safety net is a protocol, applied to every change wave:

  1. Disjoint ownership. Concurrent agents own disjoint file sets; collisions are sequenced, not merged.
  2. One adversarial verifier per deliverable. Before any pull request is armed to merge, an independent agent is instructed to break its claims — re-deriving arithmetic from committed artifacts, mutation-testing new tests for vacuity, byte-comparing vendored code against upstream, probing for information leaks and encoding drifts — and to return CONFIRMED/REFUTED verdicts with file-line evidence. A green build is treated as necessary, never sufficient.
  3. Preregistration and kill clauses. Experiments state their endpoints and abort criteria before running; two lanes this campaign were killed by their own preregistered clauses, and one abort criterion was itself corrected when measurement showed its threshold sat inside the untrained noise band.
  4. Committed evidence. Any number quoted in a report must re-derive from artifacts in the repository; forensic samples too large to regenerate are committed and covered by tests that fail if their arithmetic drifts.
  5. Paid rehearsal on real hardware. Steps 1–4 are all readings of code and artifacts, and §8.7 is the record of what they miss. Before any expensive run, the exact merged stack is deployed to a real rented box by the real launcher and observed working. Two probes and a smoke launch cost $4.11 and found 27 flaws in a stack whose four test suites were green.

Defects caught by this protocol in the reporting period include: the two withdrawn numbers of §6.4; a CI job left bodiless by a merge (which would have disabled the whole pipeline repo-wide); a trainer abort criterion that detected NaN but not infinity; an approximations-ledger identifier collision; six stale numbers in a conformance report contradicted by the JSONs beside them; an unverified assumption about poke-env's own-side reveal semantics whose failure mode was silently corrupted history channels in every real game; and — from the rehearsals of §8.7 — a trainer that never published its weights while reporting a healthy on-policy accept rate, a deploy gate that verified a file list rather than a working import, and a train/serve channel skew that would have benchmarked the week run against a blinded adapter.

Three more, from the conformance wave of §8.9, extend the taxonomy in a direction the earlier entries did not reach: an engine patch that moved the grader and was still inert in the environment it was written for, because the grader re-supplies on every turn the exact state the live defect corrupts; four new fidelity options that were reachable, tested, and absent from the training defaults table, so the wave would have changed no training physics at all had it shipped as built; and a headline decomposition quoted as measurement when only two of its three parts had an isolation run behind them. The first is the strongest single argument in this paper for step 2 of the protocol: no reading of the diff, and no green number from the differential harness, could have found it — it took an independent agent writing 27 behavioural tests directly against the engine, six of which started red.

A fourth class arrived with the doubles wave (§8.14) and it amends rule 1 rather than extending the list. Seven lanes wrote mechanics concurrently into one phase registry, owning disjoint files exactly as the protocol requires, and two of them registered handlers for the same four residual effects under different names — so the registry raised nothing and both ran, applying burn, poison, toxic and Leech Seed twice per turn, worth 37 HP of over-drain on one Pokémon in one turn. Neither lane's tests could see it: each loads only its own lane. A fifth collision of the same shape had two lanes independently counting the same Protect stall draw. Disjoint file ownership is not disjoint ownership when the shared artifact is a namespace, and the general fix is not the deletions but a wave-level test that loads every lane at once and fails on duplicate ownership of a mechanic. The same wave added the machine-checkable half: a written handler contract (§8.14) enforced by a static pass in CI, so that the constraint six concurrent authors were unknowingly relying on became a rule they could break loudly instead of quietly.

A fifth class is not new but is now a pattern, and the pattern is worth more than any of its instances: suspect the instrument before the system. Three times this campaign, the headline finding of a measurement was a defect in the thing doing the measuring. §8.14's roll-conditioned scorer forced branch index 15 of the accuracy enumeration along with damage roll 15, and its reachability check could not be moved by a real injected bug. And the crossover gauntlet of §10.2 produced, on its first run, a ~4,900-turn weather divergence that it ranked as sim2's single biggest singles gap. It was neither a gap nor weather. The fixture loader normalised identifiers by upper-casing and stripping punctuation, which is the identity map on every namespace the corpus uses except one: Showdown writes the weather condition id (raindance, sunnyday), the engine's table spells it RAIN, SUN, and nothing translated. So the simulator played 4,948 weather turns in clear skies while its own fully-implemented weather chain sat idle with nothing to apply, and the grader carried the matching hole, comparing RAINDANCE against RAIN and disagreeing on ~100% of weather turns whatever the simulator did. The two ends hid each other.

What caught it is the part that generalises. The verifier did not audit the weather code; it audited the vocabulary, tabulating resolution rates for every id space the loader touches, and read one row: weather 4,948 of 4,948 unresolved — 100.00% — against 0% for statuses, abilities, items, moves and species, and 0% for terrain, which is the natural control because it runs the identical code path and happens to be spelled Showdown-style. A 100% failure rate beside a 0% control is the signature of a missing translation table, not a missing mechanic; a real fidelity gap does not look like that. Fixing it moved the headline +4.19pp and collapsed the finding: 80% of the weather signal was the instrument, leaving 963 turns that are a real and actionable item. The same pass killed a second misreading of the same run — a 2,868-turn cluster labelled WEATHER_BOOST, which is simply the first ×1.5 hypothesis in enumeration order and therefore absorbs any ×1.5-shaped residue whatever its true cause; it moved 2.7% after every weather turn in the corpus was correctly weathered, which is how we know it was never weather. The report had read the label and skipped the in-code warning printed directly above it. And the guard the whole number rested on was vacuous: the test asserting that the graded battle carries every merged mechanic checked only that the overlay registry was a PhaseRegistry and not the default one — both true of the empty registry that is the defect it existed to catch. Mutation testing found it; a behavioural assertion replaced it.

Two rules come out of this rather than one. The first is that hygiene counters certify only what they count: this run reported zero replay errors, zero unclassified turns, zero gaps and zero truncations while its loader silently dropped a mechanic, because an unresolved identifier was none of those four things. The fix is a tripwire — the unknown-vocabulary ledger now prints above the headline — and it surfaced a genuine backlog no report had ever shown. The second is that an instrument's arithmetic being impeccable is not evidence that its input is right. Every figure in that first report re-derived exactly from its committed artifacts, and the run reproduced bit-identically; the rig was sound and the bookkeeping exact. What was wrong sat upstream of both.

A sixth class extends the fifth past the code entirely: the instrument can be the machine. §8.18 reports the same checkpoint, same file, same hash, same opponent and same 300 games scoring 44.3% on a quiet box and 54.0% on a loaded one (z = 2.37, p = 0.018), because the reference opponent is time-budgeted and background load takes its search allowance away. Every number on both sides of that comparison re-derives exactly from its committed artifacts; rules 1–4 of this protocol are satisfied completely and see nothing, because nothing in the repository is wrong. The generalisation is that artifact discipline certifies a number's derivation, not its conditions — a measurement carries its host as an unrecorded variable unless something records it — and the fixes are correspondingly outside the code: a reference arm shipped into the same load window as the arm it anchors, and separate hardware for measurement and evaluation.

And one defect found by the serving work is the cleanest example of "nothing would have noticed" in this paper. An engine patch widened the item vocabulary from 242 entries to 319. The live observation layout changed with it; not a line of the schema file did. Every trained checkpoint in existence declares 242, so each one's item embedding table is 77 rows short of the first Z-Crystal it would ever see and the training-time clamp would have folded all 77 onto a single wrong item — a silently degraded network, in production, with every test green. It was caught because the layout identifier was made to fold the vocabulary's own digest into itself, which converts the drift into a refusal at load time (§10.4).

A seventh class arrived with the GPU port, and it is the sharpest one yet: the test that passes because it cannot fail. Classes 5 and 6 say to suspect the instrument and the machine. This one says to suspect the verifier itself, and the wave produced four instances in three days, each found by a different arm of the protocol.

  • An equivalence rig that excused its own failures. The device-encoder comparison (§8.26) carried a deliberate amnesty for knife-edge cells — values sitting exactly on a comparison boundary, where a one-ULP difference legitimately flips a discrete outcome. The clause was too permissive. Injecting a >= → > mutation into a real threshold produced 82,740 excused cells and a green run: a genuine threshold bug hid inside boundary noise, which is precisely the bug class the rig existed to catch. Mutation testing found it, the clause was narrowed and paired with an excusal-rate gate, and the same injected mutation now exits non-zero with the offending cells named.
  • A comparator blind to infinity. The repo's own review pass then caught a defect the fix brief had not asked for: the comparator computed differences without an IEEE check, so a channel poisoned to all-NaN reported mismatches = 0, ok = True. A verification tool whose failure mode is silence about a totally corrupted input is worse than no tool, because it converts a loud crash into a green tick.
  • A green test that never ran. A gate test was executed as unittest … | tail, so the pipeline reported tail's exit status. The suite could fail in any way it liked and the run was green. Found by re-running under pipefail; the general form is that any test invoked through a pipe reports the last command's verdict, and the campaign now treats an un-pipefail'd test invocation as a defect in itself.
  • Two correct guards that combined into a silent skip. Two independent port lanes each added a perfectly reasonable ownership guard; merged, one lane's guard saw the other's registration and all eight of the first lane's handlers silently skipped — a union of 78 handlers where 86 were expected. Neither lane's tests could see it, because neither lane's tests load the other lane. This is §7's fourth class again (disjoint file ownership is not disjoint ownership when the shared artifact is a namespace) but one level up: the collision is now between two guards rather than two handlers, and the only thing that could see it was the union pre-grade of §8.25, run speculatively on an integration tree that was never pushed. Building a throwaway merge of five in-flight branches purely to measure them turned out to be a verification technique, not just a scheduling convenience.

The rule the four share: every one of these is a verifier reporting success, and rules 1–5 of this protocol all consume verifier verdicts. The countermeasure that actually worked in each case was the same — an injected defect that the tool is required to catch — which is mutation testing applied to the verification layer rather than to the code under test. One standing amendment came out of the wave and is recorded here because it is cheap and was learned expensively: a lane must commit its artifact directory, not merely quote headline numbers in prose. Four of six reports in one verification queue carried prose-only figures, and the one whose evidence had been torn down with its rented pod cost a 28-minute re-run to re-derive a number that had been correct all along.

An eighth class arrived on the day §8.28–§8.32 covers, and it is the one this protocol was least equipped for: suspect the reading. Classes five through seven say the instrument, the machine and the verifier can each be wrong while the code is right. This one covers the case where the code and the verifier are both right and the number, the label or the attribution carried out of them is not. §8.32 collects five instances from a single day: a benchmark that timed a cached value and reported a 170–250× speedup where the real figure is 10.8–17.1×; a dashboard that counted files instead of reading the manifest and inflated a battle total by half; a battle format inferred from an encoding width, which produced an order to the fleet that a lane refused on evidence; a defect diagnosed against a stale tree and filed as live, with a strength re-pricing implied that was never needed; and a residue-census row that priced the wave's largest named target against the wrong module. Rules 1–4 see none of these, because nothing in the repository is wrong in any of them. The countermeasure that worked in all five was the same and is cheap: require the claim to re-derive from a committed artefact, and let the owner of the code the claim is about be the one who re-derives it — rule 4 extended from numbers in reports to numbers quoted from reports.

A ninth class arrived at the masked cutover (§8.34), and the first eight are structurally blind to it: the defect only the real thing can see. Two of that night's five defects had survived every layer of this protocol — disjoint ownership, an adversarial verifier, committed artefacts, green CI across two separate merges — and both were found in the same minute, by a launch gate, on the first construction of the encoder against a live environment. One was a type-mismatched comparison (a string against a layout object) that made the device encoder unbuildable outside a test double; the other was an omitted constructor argument whose default was full information, so a run labelled masked would have trained on the opponent's hidden state under a correct-looking manifest. Neither is subtle. Both were invisible because every test that exercised the path built the thing against a stub, and a stub is exactly the object that cannot have either defect. The generalisation is uncomfortable and cheap to act on: a component whose only constructions in the test suite are synthetic has not been shown to construct at all, and the boundary where it is first built for real is a verification instrument in its own right.

The corollary is the more useful half. A gate is evidence only once it has been shown to fail. The four gates that caught these ran fail-closed before ignition, and before they were trusted they were pointed at the thing they exist to forbid — the live full-information encoder — and every one of them refused it. That is the same mutation-testing discipline §7's seventh class applies to verifiers, moved to the last artefact in the chain: a launch gate that has only ever been observed passing is indistinguishable from a launch gate that returns True. The cost of the whole mechanism was one evening of writing gates and two aborted starts; the thing it bought was the difference between a masked lineage and a cheating one wearing its label.

The round's best positive exhibit is an arm that verified its own integration recipe and refused it (§8.31). Two ways existed to wire a newly built reveal mask into the device path. The shorter one touched fewer files and would have shipped observations whose 60 tokens are masked, whose 16 history rows carry the opponent's exact HP fraction, and whose manifest declares masked: true — a leak under a correct-looking banner, which is worse than the unmasked run it replaces because the unmasked run is labelled. The mechanism was found by reading what the two enforcement paths actually share rather than by testing the recipe, which is the only way it could have been found: every test of the shorter recipe passes. Two further habits from the same arm are worth naming as protocol rather than as anecdote. A surviving mutant was published as a finding instead of being patched or excused — the gate it attacked reaches no channel in the current observation layout, so the lane proved the inertness and wrote a test that will go red the day a layout gives that field a channel, converting a hole into an expiring exemption. And a live defect found in code the lane did not own was deliberately reproduced rather than fixed, because fixing it on one side only would have left two numerically different encoders sharing one encoding id — the exact pooling-under-a-stale-identifier failure §10.4 records — so it was filed as a coordinated change with a retraining decision attached instead of smuggled in as a cleanup. The rule underneath all three: when the fix and the identity of the thing being fixed move together, they are one change, and splitting them is how incomparable data gets pooled.

8.2 Size ablation: the verdict is parity

Run 1b is a 2.49M-parameter variant (128/512/8 trunk against Run 1's 256/1024/8, ¼ the parameters) trained on an identical environment, identical hyperparameters and an identical 24-hour deadline. It is the campaign's one controlled capacity experiment, and it is the direct descendant of Phase 1's finding that capacity was the binding constraint (§4.3) — the obvious worry being that we had merely moved the ceiling rather than removed it.

We therefore conclude that 2.49M parameters already captures essentially all of the reachable policy at this experience volume, for this observation and action space, and that Phase 1's capacity ceiling was a property of a 41-column linear prior rather than of small networks generally.

The corollary is the one that matters commercially: the small net is not merely as good, it is strictly cheaper on three axes. Co-located on one H100 it generated 22% more experience per GPU-hour (289k vs 238k games/hour, §5.4). In the actor-bound regime the week run will use (§8.3), where a CPU forward pass dominates the cost of a decision rather than being amortised into a GPU batch, the gap widens to ≈1.8× — the d128 forward pass is 3.5 ms at the median against d256's 7.2 ms. And at serving time, which is the entire point of the project (§1.1), it is ≈2× cheaper per decision on the shared game-server threads. Jaxcalibur's own result points the same way: an 8.5M net trained on ≈100M games/week beat every larger, shorter-trained alternative. Given a fixed budget, buy games, not parameters.

The soaks confirm it, with one honest wrinkle. Eight 500-game soaks (§8.4) put every candidate checkpoint on a much larger denominator. Best against best, the verdict holds: Run 1's strongest soaked checkpoint is 29.0% [25.2, 33.1] and Run 1b's is 27.4% [23.7, 31.5] — a 1.6pp gap at p = 0.57. The wrinkle is that pooling all soaked arms per run reads 28.3% [26.3, 30.4] for Run 1 against 25.5% [23.6, 27.6] for Run 1b, a 2.8pp gap at p = 0.055, in the larger model's favour. We report that rather than bury it, and we do not treat it as a result, for a specific reason: the soak arm sets were not matched by design. They were checkpoint-selection soaks, so Run 1's four candidates all sat in its strong late region while Run 1b's spanned the run, including a mid-run checkpoint (2300) nominated only because a 100-game arm had flashed 33% at it. Restricting both sides to late checkpoints narrows the gap to 2.1pp at p = 0.18. The matched-design comparison remains the treadmill pooling above, at 3,500 games per side and a p of 0.54.

Our reading: parity stands, and the soak's slight lean toward capacity is a flag to watch rather than a finding.

Caveat, and a correction to earlier revisions. This is a null at one experience volume (≈6M games). It does not establish that 2.49M suffices at 30M or 100M games; capacity ceilings are volume-dependent by construction. Previous revisions said the week run would re-test it at ≈5× the volume. It did not. The run carried the 2.49M configuration alone, with no matched 8.1M arm, so it re-tested nothing about capacity — and §8.12 is careful not to read its flattening curve as the answer this ablation is still waiting for.

8.3 The week run: as built, and where the bottleneck actually was

The scaled run's design started from a measurement that reversed our intuition about what to buy. Across both verification probes (§8.7), on real rented hardware running the real training loop, the GPU was idle 0–9% of the time: 0 of 20 one-second samples non-zero on the second probe, ≈9% on the first. Generation, not learning, appeared to be the whole cost. The anchors we sized from:

Two conclusions survived contact with the real box. First, a CPU-only learner is dead: the arithmetic never closes, and a cheap GPU that sits 95% idle is still the cheapest way to run the update. Second, the topology conclusion of §8.6 sharpens: one modest GPU serves roughly 190 actor cores, so until a run exceeds that width the correct shape is one big box — actors and learner co-located, no network hop at all — rather than the distributed fleet the actor/learner layer was built to support. The layer is not wasted; it is what makes the box's actors and learner separable processes, and it is what a >190-core run will need.

A third measurement changed where we rent. Advertised vCPU counts on GPU clouds are close to fiction under a cgroup: on the probe box nproc reported 96 against a real quota of 10.2. Across rental shapes the ratio of real to advertised CPU ran ≈85% on 1–2 GPU rentals, collapsing to ≈14% on 8× bulk rentals — buying eight GPUs' worth of listed cores delivers about one and a half. Sizing a fleet off nproc would have over-provisioned the week run by 6×.

Launch

The run went live 2026-08-31 22:50Z on the 4090 box, warm-started from Run 1b checkpoint 6900 (§8.4's soak winner), 2.49M parameters, with a hard deadline-kill armed seven and a half days out. Three details of the launch path are worth recording because each was a gate that could have silently produced a wrong run:

  • The engine was built on the box, from the frozen commit, in 11.76 seconds of cargo — not copied from a build host. This closes the .so-provenance gap §8.7's smoke launch flagged: no automation could previously assert that the deployed simulator matched the checkout.
  • Shape authority worked in the direction that matters. The launch preset specified a 256-wide model; the warm-start artifact was 128-wide. The loader took its shape from the artifact and refused the preset, logging the adoption, and loaded strictly with zero rejected tensors. The failure this prevents is silent: a 256-wide net initialised from a 128-wide checkpoint would have trained happily on garbage.
  • Learner before actors, so that v0 existed before the first actor asked for weights. Publishing then ran v0→v7 with actors swapping and zero rejections.

Four defects surfaced during the launch and were fixed live. The watchdog's no-new-shards signal counted files in a directory the learner legitimately drains, and would have killed the fleet at ≈45 minutes. OMP_NUM_THREADS was unset, so 17 actors × unpinned torch produced 2,176 threads and zero shards. pkill matched its own command line, and its SIGTERM left orphan actors. And 17 actors overproduced disk by +10.5 GB/min. None was visible in any rehearsal; all four are launch-day additions to the §8.7 protocol.

The honest correction: this box is ingest-bound

The premise above — that generation is the whole cost — is wrong on this hardware, and we found out by tuning. Torch thread count was not the lever. The learner is bound by single-threaded CPython shard decode: unzip, zlib-inflate, a full SHA-256 over every byte, and two NumPy copies, ≈4.6 GB per iteration and ≈12.8 s of non-GPU time. That caps ingestion at ≈3,381 rows/s regardless of thread count or actor count, against the same box's GPU benchmark of 8,705 samples/s. The consequence is that the box supports 7 actors, not the 13–17 the core count suggests — beyond that, actors simply pile shards onto disk faster than the learner can eat them.

The 36.3 rows/s/core anchor needs a correction of its own, and it cuts the same way. That figure came from a probe running under an actor-sizing clamp of (usable − 8) // 4, i.e. four threads per actor, which SMT-thrashed. Measured properly by cgroup CPU accounting, the same cores sustain ≈103 rows/s/core. The box was never core-starved; the anchor was measuring a misconfiguration. Both corrections point the same direction: we bought the right hardware and then failed to feed it, and the binding constraint moved from silicon into our own decode path.

Fixing it mid-flight

A parallel-decode worker pool (PR #404) benchmarked at 4.5–5.6× and was deployed to the running learner, on a checkpoint boundary, with zero iterations lost — the resume path that §8.7 records being found broken on launch night had by then been fixed, and this is what it bought. Measured on the live box the gain was 1.7×, not the benchmark's 4.5×, and the gap is instructive: the benchmark had the machine to itself, while in production the decode workers compete for cores with the actors they are trying to keep up with. Learner consumption went 3,400 → 5,047 rows/s solo. Whether a dedicated learner box recovers the rest of the benched speed-up is a clean A/B and is queued as one; it is the difference between an ingest ceiling of ≈5,000 rows/s and one nearer 15,000.

Tag-team: a second box, and two findings that decided its economics

With the learner no longer the binding constraint, the reserved second box was joined — and doing so exposed a gap between what the repository documents and what it implements. The distributed layer of PR #392 "assumes one and implements none" (its own DESIGN.md, line 565): there was no courier moving shards between machines. One was written on the spot, on the box, and is queued for upstreaming. Two findings from the join are worth recording because both were economic, not technical:

  • Compression decided whether the join was worth doing at all. Uncompressed, cross-box shard traffic would have run 559 GB/hour — $2.91/hour in bandwidth, roughly seven times the second box's rent. The trajectory payload compresses ≈30×, and at --compress 6 the join costs 18.6 GB/hour and $0.10/hour. A distributed training topology can be defeated by its own egress bill before it is defeated by anything algorithmic.
  • Cross-box weight staleness eats about a quarter of the gain. Shards generated on the remote box arrive against a learner that has moved on, and roughly 25 of every 30 flagged batches get trimmed as stale. The fix is queued and cheap — halve the publish cadence so remote actors track the learner more closely — but the general lesson is that a courier's latency is a training parameter, not just an ops one.

Combined, the two boxes sustain 5,574–5,700 rows/s, and the run's ledger projects ≈25M games/week at ≈$0.86/hour across both machines. Stated plainly: the join is a speed win, not a cost win. Cost per million games went from $4.92 (pre-#404, ingest-bound) to $3.29 (post-#404, one box) and then back up to $3.63 on the join, because the second box's rent and its stale-trim losses together outrun its marginal throughput. We bought wall-clock, knowingly, and the ledger says so rather than the press release.

At the snapshot of §8.8 the run had produced 951,343 self-play games in its first five hours, with the learner consuming 5,551 rows/s against 5,884 rows/s of generation.

8.5 What the Losses Taught Us — and one finding withdrawn

This section reported three mechanisms in earlier revisions. A second autopsy, run on data the first one did not have, confirms and sharpens one, changes the instrument on another, and withdraws the third. The withdrawal is reported in place rather than edited out of history.

The instrument matters, so it is stated first. The original autopsy was a hand audit over ≈1,400 transfer battles with no per-decision trace of what the network believed. The second runs over 44 deep-evaluation arms whose harness logs the network's own value and post-mask policy on every decision: 6,990 battles in the "recent" cohort alone (checkpoints from iteration 1834 on, aggregate 38.7%), 137k of our decisions against 122k of Foul Play's. Where the newer instrument is better we take its answer; where it is merely different we say so rather than quietly restating.

Comparing this run's early checkpoints against its recent ones, every bucket improved by a consistent +2 to +4 points on 10k–35k decisions per bucket per era: real healing, over ten thousand games of experience, and far too slow to close a twenty-point gap inside the campaign's decision horizon. This is now the best-evidenced defect in the system and the one most worth a targeted intervention — an auxiliary calibration objective, or a temperature on the value head — rather than waiting out.

The behavioural consequence, on the same data: 70.9% of recent losses (2,478 of 3,495) had the network reporting ≥55% win probability at some point past the opening, and lost anyway. That is inverted from the 76% never-competitive / 24% thrown split this section previously carried, and the inversion is an instrument change rather than a discovery: the old split was computed from HP-swing proxies, the new one from the value head's own self-report. Read against the calibration table the two are consistent — the self-report is precisely the thing that is miscalibrated, so a split defined by it must find more "thrown" battles. What survives the change of instrument is the direction: most current losses are ones the network believed it was winning.

The dominant loss shape is a snowball. A representative 38-turn battle: value 0.968 at turn 24 on a recoil move, then 0.725, 0.662, 0.520, 0.434, 0.474, 0.509, 0.162, 0.117, 0.094, 0.024 as four party members were forced out in succession over five turns by a single opposing threat for which no check was ever found. A weaker secondary motif — four or more consecutive attacking decisions with monotonically declining value and no pivot — appears in 35.7% of losses against 28.1% of wins, a real but modest 7.6-point discrimination. And one motif is reported precisely because it failed: "held a ≥0.6 value and that Pokémon then died" occurs in 90.9% of wins and 74.9% of losses — more common in wins. In a format where both sides trade Pokémon constantly that is a description of a long game, not of a loss, and it is exactly the shape of diagnostic that looks compelling until someone runs the win-rate control.

Earlier revisions of this section carried the claim that the agent "switches on 8% of its decisions against Foul Play's 15%," holds ≈1% of policy mass on switch actions, and does not switch more when losing — and treated it as a structural cap and one of three named mechanisms behind the plateau. It does not replicate. We withdraw it. Measured apples-to-apples on the current data, excluding forced replacements after a faint and the follow-up to one's own pivot move, from both sides:

On the comparable denominator we switch about 2.6× more often than Foul Play does, not less; on the naive one the two sides are statistically indistinguishable. Neither denominator, nor any mixture of the two that was tried, reproduces 8% and 15%. The original figures are best explained as our voluntary rate compared against Foul Play's raw one — arithmetically correct on each side, and meaningless across them. The residual uncertainty runs the safe way: Foul Play's forced-versus-voluntary split is inferred from its search's own root summaries rather than from a ground-truth flag, which makes 3.9% a lower bound, so that inference would have to be nearly threefold wrong before the original claim came back.

This is not a bookkeeping correction. The withdrawn finding was load-bearing — it named a defect, it explained a plateau, and it pointed engineering effort at switch behaviour. Two things replace it. A real secondary gap survives in the neighbourhood: Foul Play's forced/pivot fraction is 21.8% against our 14.9%, i.e. it uses pivot moves — U-turn, Volt Switch and relatives — markedly more than we do. That is a different mechanism with a different fix, and it has not yet been sized. And the value-bleed motif above (35.7% vs 28.1%) is a better-targeted, if modest, replacement signal than any aggregate rate comparison.

The rule this produced sits beside §5.2's rule about sizing a class from the same sample used to diagnose it: an aggregate rate compared across two systems is a finding only if both sides are measured on the same denominator by the same instrument. Nothing in the pipeline would have caught the original number, because it was not wrong — it was incomparable.

Unchanged: the observation defect. Immunity-into-effect attacks never improved across training, and the root cause was an observation defect rather than a policy failure: eight event-outcome channels (miss, crit, no-effect, denied, for each side) were hardwired to zero in every observation the network ever saw, so no learning signal ever reported that an attack had failed. Six are now live (§8.6). Separately, redundant status and hazard moves — re-applying an effect already up — occur 16–31 times per 100 battles: wasted actions rather than losing ones, but evidence that the policy does not track field state precisely.

Caveat, and a first bound on it. The share of losses attributable to variance rather than misplay (a crit or a miss inside an otherwise sound line) still has only a ceiling, and it is measured on the older 100-battle protocol-stream sample, not on this autopsy. Critical-hit rates match the generation-9 base rate on both sides — ours 3.92%, the opponent's 4.07%, against a nominal 4.17% — which rules out an engine anomaly as an explanation for the gap. 18.2% of our losses contain two or more opponent critical hits, a loose ceiling on that form of variance, not an estimate of it: a battle can contain two crits and still be lost on merit. Turning the ceiling into a decomposition needs the outcome stream, and the deep-evaluation harness that produced everything else in this section runs with protocol_log off on every arm — so the request that follows from this autopsy is an instrumentation request, not an analysis one.

8.6 Closing the Sim-to-Real Gap

Six pull requests merged in one night, aimed directly at defects the loss autopsy and the conformance ledger surfaced; each carried an independent adversarial verifier (§7), and roughly twenty defects were caught before merge. The three that changed measurable quantities, with the re-measurements that have since landed:

  • PR #391 — protocol-stream opponent observation (closes approximation A9) + legal-mask fallback. Replaces inference of the opponent's hidden state with a direct read of Showdown's protocol stream, targeting the ≈55% of decisions on which opponent actions were previously unobserved (§6.2); also hardens the legal-mask fallback path implicated in the §6.4 withdrawn result. Re-measured: unknown-action rate 56.4% → 19.8%, residual decomposed and found benign, A9 closed (§6.2).
  • PR #392 — distributed actor/learner layer. Splits environment rollout from the learner process. Measured at ≈70 KB/sample, projecting to ≈51 MB/s of actor→learner traffic at full training rate. Our original conclusion — actors must sit in the same datacenter as the learner — survives, but the probes of §8.7 sharpened it into something stronger and cheaper: because the GPU idles at 0–9% and one modest learner feeds ≈190 actor cores (§8.3), a run below that width should not cross a network at all. The correct default is one box, not a fleet; the distributed layer earns its keep only above ≈190 actor cores, and meanwhile provides the process separation the box itself uses.
  • PR #393/#394 — six dead event channels, and conformance patches 18–21. Eight of 68 event-history channels (miss, crit, no-effect, denied, for each side) had been hardwired to zero in every observation Runs 1 and 1b ever saw — the proven root cause of §8.5's never-improving immunity blunders. Six are now live. The engine patches fix the damage-roll application order, PP-decrement timing, Curse's target selection, a status duration-counter wire bug, and Ice Face / Sturdy / Spikes / Defog interactions. Re-measured: turn agreement 79.56% → 85.73% [85.46, 86.00] over 986 battles and 66,034 turns; reward agreement 92.09%; PP class −86%; Curse closed (§5.2).

Two cautions attach to this section. First, none of §8.4's numbers reflect any of it — Runs 1 and 1b trained against the 17-patch engine with the six channels dead. Any strength attributable to these changes appears only in the week run, which is the first to train on them. Second, a train/serve skew was found and closed between those PRs and the week run: the six new channels were live in the training environment but zeroed by the evaluation adapter, so the two paths disagreed 66-derived/2-zeroed against 66-derived/8-zeroed. Both paths now report an identical census, verified independently on two live runs (§8.7). Had this shipped, the week run would have trained a policy on signals its own benchmark could not see.

This wave has a successor, and the pattern repeated. §8.9 is the same exercise run a second time — analysis over the live run's own data, a build wave, an adversarial verification pass — and it found the same class of gap in a new place: four new fidelity options that were reachable, tested, and absent from the training defaults table, i.e. correct code that would have changed nothing about what the network trained against. The recurring shape across both waves is not a wrong fix but a right fix that never reaches the run, and it is now pinned by a test in general form rather than by a checklist.

8.7 Verifying the Stack Before Spending a Week on It

A week-long run costs more than everything in this paper combined and produces one number. Before committing it we ran a deliberate verification campaign on rented hardware, on the premise that green test suites certify the code we thought to test and nothing else. The campaign cost $4.11 — 0.7% of the run it was protecting — and found 27 flaws.

Probe 1 ($2.54, 309,350 games on a rented 4090). The science stack worked: 19.97M samples with zero encoding or layout mismatches, zero quarantined or corrupt shards, all nine fidelity options on, warm start functional, and a byte-exact 70,298 bytes per row matching the environment contract. The operational stack did not. Thirteen flaws, of which the one that matters for this paper is F6: the trainer never published its weights, and had no flag to make it. Actors therefore ran against the initial parameters for the entire probe while the accept-rate metric read a healthy 1.0. The consequence is a caveat we are obliged to state loudly:

Probe 1's flat learning curve is not a learning result. Its training signal was entirely off-policy by construction, and its evaluation adapter was additionally missing six channels the trainer had. The pooled 13.67% [10.24, 18.02] it measured, and its "late-vs-base +0.83pt, p = 0.74" slope read, say nothing about whether the stack learns. They are reported here as a defect report, not as evidence. The collapse of its opponent-action auxiliary head from 0.704 to 0.46 across the probe is a symptom of the same defect.

Probe 1 also corrected a number we had been carrying: Run 1b's iteration-200 checkpoint is ≈12–14% against Foul Play, not the ≈24% a dashboard reading had suggested — the 24% band belongs to much later checkpoints. §8.1's 12.0% is the corrected figure.

Probe 2 ($0.86, on the fixed stack). Eight of nine claimed fixes verified in use rather than in diff. The weight loop demonstrably closed: the learner published v0 before the first gradient step and then v0→v8 with eight distinct SHA-256 digests, actors were observed swapping to v1 and v2, zero rejected. Integrity counters were proven live rather than merely zero by feeding the ingest path a fabricated encoding id and confirming it refused the shard. Fourteen new flaws surfaced, the launch-gating one being instructive:

The deploy gate reported 11/11 required files present, and then every actor died on ImportError. The gate checked a curated list of files; the encoder's import closure reached three directories outside the deployed tree. Probe 1 had masked it by hand-deploying a wider tree. A gate that checks a list of files is not a gate that checks the program runs — the fix makes the remote gate import and construct the encoder. A fifth external dependency, a JSON data file opened lazily during construction, was found only because of that change: a bare import would still have passed.

Fix waves and close-out. PRs #397–399 closed probe 1's findings, #400–401 closed probe 2's; roughly thirteen further defects were caught by adversarial verifiers across five review rounds on those two PRs alone, including one where a verifier's own re-derivation found that a claimed fix had not landed. A final $0.71 smoke launch then exercised the exact merged stack end to end on a fresh rental — rent, gate, warm start, publish v0, actor hot-swap, heartbeat aggregate, terminate — and passed 10 of 10 checks.

Two more, from the measurement harness and from launch night. The soaks of §8.4 surfaced a liveness defect the probes could not have: an upstream engine panic (Invalid PokemonIndex: 6) killed a Foul Play seat mid-series, and our seat then wedged permanently, challenging an opponent that no longer existed — silently converting a 500-game arm into a 426-game one. PR #402 answers it with bounded challenge waits, paired seat supervision so a dead partner is detected rather than waited on, and series resume. PR #403 has a better story still: the checkpoint-resume path was broken by a missing key in the published-parameters blob, and it was discovered on production launch night, by three independent agents hitting the same failure from three different directions. A safety net nobody had exercised was not a safety net; the fix and its verifier round landed the same evening.

Launch day is now part of the protocol. The rehearsal of §8.7 validated everything it was designed to validate and still did not catch the four defects that appeared when the real week run started on the real box (§8.3): a watchdog counting files in a directory the learner drains, an unset OMP_NUM_THREADS producing 2,176 threads and zero output, a pkill matching its own command line, and a producer/consumer imbalance that would have filled the disk. The extension we adopted is that the first hour of a long run is treated as the last stage of verification — instrumented, watched by an agent whose job is to look for what is wrong rather than to confirm what is right, and expected to yield defects rather than hoped to be clean.

The cadence held through the live run. Five further pull requests shipped while the week run was executing, each carrying the same adversarial verifier round, and roughly fifteen more findings were closed before merge: #402 (evaluation-seat liveness — the wedge that truncated two soaks), #403 (the resume-format defect, plus a SIGTERM checkpoint handler and a drain-aware stall guard), #404 (parallel ingest, benched 4.5–5.6×, verified "safe to deploy" against adversarial shard ordering and byte-identity before it was pointed at the live learner), #405 (the GPU compute plan), and #406 (the research archive). Deploying #404 into a running production learner is the strongest thing we can say for the protocol: the change was made to a system that had been training for hours, on a checkpoint boundary, and cost nothing.

What we take from all of this. Every defect above was invisible to a green build, and most were invisible to the diff as well; they appeared only when real code was deployed to a real box by the real launcher. Three classes recurred: gates that verify an artifact rather than a behaviour (the file-list gate, the accept-rate metric reading 1.0 against unpublished weights); metrics that flatter themselves (a consumption rate reported as a generation rate, swinging 7× while draining a backlog; and, later, an actor-throughput anchor that was really measuring an SMT-thrashing misconfiguration — §8.3); and safety nets nobody has pulled (the resume path, the seat-liveness supervisor, the SIGTERM handler whose absence cost two runs their final checkpoints). The protocol change we adopted: no long run launches without a paid end-to-end rehearsal of the exact merged stack on the exact target shape; no fix counts as landed until it is observed working on rented hardware; and no recovery mechanism counts as existing until it has been deliberately triggered. The rehearsal is cheap; a week of off-policy training is not.

8.9 Engine conformance wave 2: audit, build, adversarial verification, live cut — one day

On 2026-09-01 the campaign ran a complete conformance cycle inside a single day: three read-only analysis lanes over the week run's own data, a build wave against their ranked findings, an adversarial verification pass over the resulting diff, and a deployment into the running training fleet by evening. This section reports the measurement and what verifying it taught us; §8.11 reports the deployment as an experiment in its own right.

The corpus discontinuity, stated before the result. The 986-battle corpus behind §5.2's 85.73% no longer exists: it was generated on a rented pod that was terminated at the end of its run, and only the report and its JSON survive. What the physics half buys is small, and it was verified against the engine rather than the scoreboard. Patch 24 is the clean example. A Taunt landing on a Pokémon that switched in the same turn denies status moves on two subsequent selection turns in the pinned oracle, three in our engine with the option off, and two with it on. That is a correction to physics a training run feels, not to a number a grader reads — and it is a direct behavioural test of the engine, not an inference from a wire delta.

Two negative results, reported because they cost the same as the positive ones. Minior's Shields Down forme flip was implemented, measured, and reverted: adding the missing switch-in hook made the engine flip correctly, moved agreement +0.000pp, and made one class worse by nine turns, because Showdown restores the seeded colour species (miniorviolet) and this engine can only name the generic form. The fix needs a base-species channel and a regenerated corpus, not a hook, and a behaviourally-correct change that trades one mismatch shape for another is not something to slip past a reviewer under a green headline. Separately, the once-per-battle ability boosts (Intrepid Sword, Dauntless Shield) were left at a design note for a reason worth recording: the dumper does not record the flag, and the comparator rebuilds the engine from Showdown's canonical state every turn, so the patch would move this corpus by exactly zero while being a genuine fix for continuous play. The blocker there is the measuring instrument, not the engine.

The verification exhibit: a fix the grader structurally could not see. Patch 26 made Disable restrict a move for the first time — the volatile had been applied for the whole campaign and never read by the move-legality function, so the move was a complete no-op. The adversarial pass then found that it was still a no-op where it mattered. The engine only maintains its last_used_move tracker when a position contains one of five named moves, and Disable is not among them; on any team without those five the tracker stays empty, the patch's onTryHit equivalent refuses every Disable, and the move remains exactly the no-op the patch was written to end.

The conformance harness cannot see this, by construction. It is a transition-mode replay: it deserializes last_used_move off the wire on every turn, so the state the live defect corrupts is reset before the comparator can ever observe it. Continuous play — a self-play training run, or the shipped in-game search — does not, and continuous play is the deployment this patch was written for. The measurement that makes the point is an identity rather than a difference: three separate gauntlet runs spanning the fix scored byte-identically — 57,072 of 65,321 turns agreeing, 2,155 unclassified, all fourteen class counts equal. That identity is the required result, not a suspicious one. Every defect the verification pass closed is invisible to this harness on purpose: the inert-Disable fix is masked by the wire re-supply; a duration leak needs its fidelity option off and the harness runs it on; a newly-reachable "no legal move" position needs the engine to derive legality itself, and the harness hands it Showdown's own disabled flags each turn. A grader that cannot move is the correct grader here, and reading a moved number as evidence of the fix working would have been the wrong conclusion.

The general form, and it is the strongest statement this paper can make about §7's protocol: a differential harness scores the physics it re-seeds every turn; defects that exist only across turns are outside its field of view. Only a behavioural test or a live run sees those. The verification pass wrote 27 direct behavioural tests against the engine for exactly that reason, six of which started red.

Three further defects, and one deployment gate. Beyond the inert-Disable finding the pass closed a volatile-duration leak that survived a switch with the option off; a newly reachable "no legal move, and this engine has no Struggle" position created by Disable interacting with Choice-item locking, fixed with a documented divergence from Showdown rather than a match to it (re-offer the locked move, because the alternative is the Pokémon passing its turn for free — a bigger lie, and one a search would learn to farm); and an options-default test that had quietly fallen to covering 9 of 13 flags. The gate is the one that would have made the whole wave cosmetic: the four new fidelity options were reachable and tested but absent from the training defaults table, and every option is written explicitly rather than inherited, so an omission is a hard OFF. Shipped as built, the wave would have changed no training physics whatsoever. Two pins now fail if a future wave repeats it, one of them in general form — any option that is neither in the defaults table nor owned by a constructor argument fails the test. Suites at close-out: 942 vendor tests, 75 native, 52 harness Python, all green.

One residual honesty item the wave could not close: the comparator's report JSON carries no per-turn agreement vector, so "no regression is hiding inside the net gain" is argued by mechanism — every class that rose is a mechanic no patch touched — rather than checked by set difference. That is a ~20-line harness change and it is queued, because it retires the whole class of question.

8.10 The search multiplier at the serving budget: a measured zero

Phase 1 of this project was a search system, and Jaxcalibur's headline finding is that inference-time search adds +100–150 Elo on top of a trained network's intuition. Once the network alone passed the search system (§8.4), the obvious question was whether wrapping the network in a search multiplies it. We built the search and measured it against the same checkpoint, the same opponent, and the same decision budget. The multiplier is zero.

The build, because it determines what the number means. The training path drives the Rust engine over a C ABI; the live path reads a poke-env battle and writes the same state-field schema; the one missing edge was schema → engine wire, so a position the live adapter is looking at could be handed back to the engine and played forward. That edge is now written and, crucially, is checkable rather than arguable: a live position written to the wire, seeded into a fresh environment, and compared field by field round-trips 128 of 128 positions byte-identically. Two of the author's initial guesses about the wire format were wrong and were caught by that test; both would have silently corrupted every rollout.

Four properties of the search are worth stating because each bounds the result. Hidden information is determinized from the engine's own random-battle builder — the same set data the network trained against, 12,288 donor Pokémon covering all 509 species — because handing the engine the live adapter's honest zeros for an unseen opponent produces not a weak opponent but an incoherent one (a party of blanks reads as nearly wiped, and every rollout returns a win). The opponent's reply comes from the network's own opponent-action auxiliary head, which is trained for exactly this prediction and is free, rather than from a second forward pass. The tree is one ply deep, which is an ABI limit and not a shortcut: the environment can be seeded from a wire at construction and stepped, but has no state-restore call, so an interior node cannot be revisited. And a trapped position cannot be seeded at all, so it falls back to the greedy answer — 31 of 10,173 decisions, 0.30%, every one of them that case.

A 100 ms budget buys ≈37 simulations per decision, because a CPU forward pass costs ≈10 ms fixed plus ≈1.3 ms per row, and 37 simulations spread over six to nine legal actions cannot outvote a confident policy prior — PUCT's prior term dominates and the mean values never separate. Two known defects were left unfixed so that the shipped code is exactly the code that produced these numbers, and both bias toward the search: a round-cost estimator that let the matched arm overrun to a 110 ms mean against its 100 ms budget, and a determinizer that overwrites a revealed level (harmless in this format, where a same-species donor almost always agrees). The result reads zero anyway.

What we take from it, stated as the conclusion the build itself reached. A search is only as good as the value function it backs up, and §8.5 has just measured this value function as overconfident by up to 24 points through its middle range. Improving the evaluator dominates improving the search. Two consequences follow. First, no more search tuning — c_puct, depth, world count — until the simulation budget moves by an order of magnitude; the finding is a property of the budget and the depth, not of the parameters. Second, and this is a claim we are deliberately retiring rather than making: an earlier draft of the build report argued that the GPU-inference programme (§10) was the enabling condition for search. The completed 18× arm does not support that. Cheaper inference is worth buying for game generation; it should not be sold on the promise that search converts it into strength, because the one measurement we have of that conversion is consistent with zero.

And the product constraint (§1.1) rules on the rest: even the arm that acts costs 45 s/game against the greedy 9.2, on a system whose entire design target is 500 concurrent battles on shared threads. The matched arm is nearly free in wall clock (10.0 s/game, because a Showdown game is dominated by waiting on the opponent's own search) — but wall clock is not the axis that binds. Simulations per millisecond is, and that is the axis on which this arm has nothing to spend.

8.11 The mid-run physics cut, as a pre-registered natural experiment

Wave 2 (§8.9) was cut into the live week run at 2026-09-01T18:21:34Z, iteration 4076, weights version 825, with no rollback. This section records the deployment and then states, before the outcome exists, what we expect it to show.

The deployment. Eighteen actors across two boxes were rolled onto the rebuilt engine in batches, and the verification signal was chosen so that it could not be satisfied by a log line: cargo writes a new inode, so any process still holding the old library shows it as (deleted) in its own memory map. Every restarted actor was checked against the live inode; the final state was 11 of 11 and 7 of 7 on the new engine, zero on the old. All thirteen fidelity options were confirmed by reading them back out of the native library rather than echoing what was requested — the environment constructor asks the library for its own option list and refuses to proceed if it disagrees with the Python contract, which makes construction itself the strongest available check.

The learner took one SIGTERM, to an exact recorded PID rather than a pattern match, after confirming from the running code that its graceful-stop handler existed. That handler is the one whose absence cost Runs 1 and 1b their final checkpoints (§8.4); this is the first time it has been used in anger. It finished iteration 4076, wrote a boundary checkpoint, and exited in 12 seconds; the relaunch resumed at 4077 with the weights version continuing 825 → 827 → 829, i.e. zero iterations lost. The relaunch also carried the publish-cadence change §8.3 queued for cross-box staleness (--publish-every 5 → 10). Fifteen minutes after the cut: no PID churn, no respawns, entropy 0.747–0.774 inside its healthy band, quarantine and layout-mismatch counters at zero, the second box mirroring weights in step, and the deep-evaluation program running across the seam untouched. Rollback assets — the pre-cut library, the pre-cut harness tree, and a learner relaunch script — were staged and went unused.

One thing the deployment could not establish, and it is recorded rather than smoothed: the runtime trees on both boxes are extracted tarballs with no .git, so before this cut the deployed commit was not knowable from the box. It is now, because the cut shipped the whole of merged main as one tree — which is itself a consistency win, and the reason the provenance gap of §8.3 is finally closed at both ends.

Why this is an experiment. Training physics changed while the measuring instrument did not. The evaluation opponent is real Foul Play in real Showdown battles, unchanged across the seam, and both rigs of §8.8 evaluate checkpoints continuously on either side of it. Checkpoints before and after iteration 4076 therefore form a before/after comparison with the ruler held fixed — the cleanest natural experiment this campaign has had, and one that costs nothing extra because both arms were going to be evaluated anyway.

Pre-registered expectation, written before the outcome. If wave 1's precedent holds, we expect (i) a brief flat-or-dip window as the policy unlearns habits that were only correct in the old simulator — anything acquired while Disable was a no-op, while a Substitute's HP survived its owner leaving the field, while Taunt ran a turn long — followed by (ii) a steeper climb than the pre-cut grind, which §8.8 measures at roughly +4pp over nineteen hours. We pre-commit to three ways of being wrong. No visible dip is consistent with the corrections being too small to matter at this policy's level. A dip with no recovery would say the patched physics is harder rather than truer. And a smooth continuation with neither feature would say that training-environment fidelity is simply not the binding constraint on this curve — which would be the most informative of the three outcomes, and would move §9's list more than a confirmation would.

We are also stating the size prior, so that a null is not later re-read as a disappointment: the four patches are worth ≈+0.5pp of turn agreement in physics terms (§8.9), the rest of the wave's headline being a reporting correction that changes nothing about how the engine plays. That is small next to the step wave 1 produced, and we are predicting the shape of the response, not its magnitude.

Outcome, filled. The seam is now bracketed by 55 pre-cut and 28 post-cut deep-evaluation arms, and the honest answer is the third of the three we pre-committed to: no dip, and no step distinguishable from the climb that was already happening.

The first post-cut arm (checkpoint 4099, 23 iterations past the seam) came in at 43.3%, above the pre-cut band rather than below it, so expectation (i) — a brief unlearning dip — did not occur at the resolution we can measure. The campaign's own planning document anticipated this exact misreading and carries it as a numbered risk. So the honest summary is: the run rose from an inherited 27.4% to a sustained ≈41–42%, peaked at 47.7%, and then stopped improving for its final third, and why it stopped is not established by this run. pending A matched capacity re-test at this experience volume — the experiment that would actually decide it. What the flattening did decide was operational: a curve that is not moving is not worth seven days of rented hardware, so the run was banked and the hardware repointed (§10).

The BCE cut is reported without a result, on purpose, and the reason is the most useful thing in this section. §8.5's headline defect is a value head overconfident by up to 24 points; the obvious intervention is to train it with a proper scoring rule, so the value objective was switched to binary cross-entropy at iteration 4447. An offline re-check on post-cut decisions then had to answer whether calibration had improved. Its verdict, quoted rather than paraphrased:

Underpowered for the buckets that matter — no calibration verdict is warranted yet, in either direction.

Two things produce that verdict, and both are methodological rather than about Pokémon. The first is a clustering error we caught in our own analysis: roughly five logged decisions come from each battle and share that battle's single win/loss label, so decision-level significance tests treat one observation as five. Computed that way the headline buckets read p = 0.0017 and p = 0.018 and look like a result. Corrected for battle-level clustering the same comparisons read p = 0.57 and p = 0.39 — nothing. We report the invalid numbers next to the valid ones because the gap between them is the lesson: the temptation to read the decision-level p-value is exactly proportional to how much you want the intervention to have worked.

The second is a drift confound with a measured size. Calibration in these buckets has historically healed by +2 to +4 points per run on its own, from experience alone (§8.5 measures precisely this). The current post-cut sample is powered to detect a shift of ≥ +5 points. A genuine +3-point win from BCE and ordinary drift are therefore the same observation at this n, and no honest reading separates them. The cut stays in — it is theoretically better-motivated than MSE and costs nothing — but the paper records no strength or calibration claim for it, and will not until a powered comparison exists. pending A powered BCE calibration read.

The bf16 cut is a clean engineering number and a good illustration of Amdahl. Mixed precision in the learner's update was projected at 1.82× on the update phase and delivered 1.49× there; on the whole training iteration the projection was 1.47× and the delivery 1.31× (15.35 s → 11.59 s). The shortfall is entirely accounted for: the 4.34-second ingest phase was untouched and, having not got faster, now occupies ≈37% of the iteration. This is a learner-side number — actors cannot see it — and we state that because "1.31× faster" invites being read as 1.31× more games.

The serving fold is where the throughput actually came from. The week run's actors each ran a CPU forward pass per decision (§8.3's actor-bound regime). Moving that forward onto a single consumer GPU was first measured as a same-box A/B — three GPU-served actors against four CPU-forward controls, running at the same time on the same machine — and read 2.23× the rows/second per actor on 0.49× the cores, i.e. 4.51× rows/second per core. Notably the whole of the 2.23× came from moving the forward to the GPU rather than from batching, which is why it was worth folding out rather than tuning. Folded from K = 3 to K = 7 served actors, the fleet went 8,173 → 11,115 rows/s (+36.0%) overall and 3,731 → 6,665 rows/s (+78.6%) on the box that had the GPU. The two numbers measure different things and we keep them apart: 2.23× is per-actor in a controlled A/B, +36% is the fleet before and after the expansion.

What the four changes together demonstrate. Every one of them was applied to a training run that had been executing for hours, at a checkpoint boundary, with zero iterations lost, using the graceful stop path whose absence cost Runs 1 and 1b their final checkpoints (§8.4). That capability was built as an ops convenience and turned out to be a research instrument: it is what allows a campaign to test an intervention against its own live baseline instead of waiting for the next run. The cost is stated in §9 — a run that absorbs four treatments is not a single-treatment curve, and §8.12's plateau claim is made in spite of that rather than in ignorance of it.

8.14 Doubles: a simulator in a day, and the metric that made it honest

§9 has listed doubles as "the eventual differentiator, requiring engine extension before any training run" since the first revision of this paper. That extension now exists in draft. sim2 is a second simulator — written in Python over fixed-shape arrays rather than as a patch to the Rust fork — built by seven concurrent agent lanes in roughly one day and graded, from its first commit, against the same pinned Showdown oracle the singles engine answers to.

Why a second simulator rather than an extension of the first. Doubles needs a slot axis through every mechanic (targeting, redirection, spread damage, speed order across four positions), and the Rust fork's singles shape is load-bearing throughout. Writing the doubles engine fresh also let it be written GPU-shaped from the first line — fixed-shape arrays, branch-enumerate-and-mask, no data-dependent control flow — which is the open call §10 recorded in the previous revision. That call has been answered in the affirmative, and §8.15 reports what it bought.

The measurement problem, and the metric we adopted. A doubles turn's damage depends on which of Showdown's 16 damage rolls was drawn, and the event structure is itself roll-dependent: a knockout at roll 15 that does not happen at roll 3 removes a position and changes what the next attacker in the same turn is even able to hit. Scored at one forced roll, a simulator with perfect formulas is charged forever for every turn whose oracle roll differed — the metric cannot reach 100% by construction. So the corpus is replayed once per roll, all sixteen, and a turn counts as agreeing if any replay both matched every non-HP field and explained every Pokémon's HP. The bar this sets is the honest one: dice are excused, logic never is. A turn whose targets[] or execution order diverges is not counted no matter what its HP does.

The scorer was itself a source of defects, and three were caught before any lane's number was believed. Forcing "roll 15" for maximum damage also forced branch index 15 of the accuracy and speed-order enumerations, clamping every move to MISS and reversing tie-breaks — the original baseline was measured through that. The reachability check did not subtract the simulator's own residual damage before comparing, which a verifier proved by injecting a fabricated maxhp/16 residual: it moved the plain grader from 107 to 146 disagreeing turns and moved the roll-conditioned headline by exactly zero, which is a defect, not a robustness result. Fixing it moved the metric 43/146 → 44/146 at an unchanged commit. A grader that cannot be moved by a real injected bug is broken in the same way a grader that moves for no reason is.

The numbers, kept in their own series. The wave's baseline was measured on the older plain turn-agreement metric and the roll-conditioned metric did not exist until mid-wave, so these are two series and this paper does not splice them (the same rule §5.2 applies to the 85.934/85.733 discontinuity):

The methods contribution is the handler contract. Seven lanes writing mechanics into one phase registry, concurrently, in one day, is precisely the situation §7's disjoint-ownership rule was written for — and file-level disjointness is not enough when the shared artifact is a registry rather than a file. Two mechanisms carried it.

The first is HANDLERS.md: seven rules (H1–H7) stating what a handler may not do — branch on data, take a value-dependent shape, loop over the batch, write an array in place, call an in-place NumPy API, do host bookkeeping, or name a NumPy-only dtype. These are exactly the things that will not jit-trace, and the contract exists because the JAX transcription of §8.15 succeeded only because the original core happened to have been written to an unwritten rule that six new lanes were about to break. The contract is enforced by a static AST pass inside the existing CI job — no new job, no new dependency — which discovers handlers from their registration calls and follows helper calls transitively, on the principle that a rule broken in a helper is still your rule broken; where JAX is importable it additionally traces each handler for real. Bringing main into compliance fixed 24 violations in one module with byte-identical gauntlet output before and after.

The second is what the verifiers caught, and it is the strongest argument in this wave for §7. Four residual effects were being applied twice per turn — burn, poison, toxic and Leech Seed — because the field lane and the status lane each registered handlers for them under different names. Different names mean the registry raises nothing and both run. Neither lane's test suite could see it, because each loads only its own lane. Reproduced on real damage, it over-drained one Pokémon by 37 HP in a single turn. A fifth collision of the same shape was then found between the specials and status lanes, which had independently tracked the same Protect stall counter and let the two copies drift apart. The general fix is not the four deletions; it is a wave-level cross-lane test that loads every lane together and fails on duplicate ownership of a mechanic. Concurrent agents can own disjoint files and still collide on a shared namespace, and only a test that loads all of their work at once will say so.

8.15 The GPU simulator, and what an H100 actually buys

The long-run plan (§10) has always ended at a GPU-resident simulator — Jaxcalibur's configuration, and the only known way to reach ≈100M games/week. This revision reports the first real measurements of that path, and the most useful of them is an economic negative.

The port. sim2 — the doubles simulator of §8.14 — was transcribed to JAX as fixed-shape arrays with a branch-enumerate-and-mask discipline, so that the same code runs on CPU and GPU. Equivalence was established the only way worth establishing it: ndarray.tobytes() over all 67 array_state fields and 7 slotstate fields, at kernel, step and rollout level, across five configurations (two hosts × CPU/GPU, either side of a schema seam). 1,044 step cases and 2,450 kernel checks, zero disagreements.

That guarantee has since lapsed, and we say so rather than keep quoting it. The port was proven bit-identical against a commit, not against a moving target, and §8.14's mechanics lanes then landed underneath it. Re-run against current main, the port fails 105 of 146 fixture records and 28 of 78 fields — the drift starts at move_pp, off by exactly one. The failure signature is identical on CPU, so this is staleness, not a GPU numerics problem. It is disclosed in the test suite as expectedFailure with an agreement floor that fails if fewer than 41 of 146 records still match byte-for-byte, rather than skipped or deleted; re-synchronising the port is scheduled work that has not started. The paper's claim is therefore: the transcription method was proven exact once, and the port is currently behind the simulator it was transcribed from.

Throughput, on the card we own. On an RTX 4080 SUPER, rolling 32 turns into a single jit via lax.fori_loop, the port peaks at 31.0M battle-turns/second at a batch of 65,536 — against the same box's single-threaded numpy core at 64,353 turns/second, a 481× ratio. Both are peaks, each at its own best batch; at a matched batch of 16,000 and one turn per launch the honest ratio is 178×. We quote within-run ratios only, because a shared card's run-to-run variance is real.

The diagnosis is measured, not inferred, and it is the reason the result generalises. What this cost, and what it saved. The probe was sixty minutes of a single H100 at $3.17/hour — $3.17 — and it was run specifically instead of committing a training run to rented H100 capacity. An earlier 4×H100 rental at $6.50/hour had already been destroyed without yielding data when its host never came up, so the campaign had reason to buy the cheap answer first. Stated as the rule we now hold: a device comparison is a measurement, not a spec-sheet lookup, and at $3 it is cheaper than one hour of being wrong.

The fix is arithmetic, and it is now measured. The probe answered "can a faster device rescue a launch-bound kernel" — no — but not "why is it launch-bound." Profiling one compiled step at B = 1,024 attributed 404 entry dispatches, of which 168 were scatters. 168 was not an estimate: it is exactly the number of .at[index].set(...) call sites the step contains, XLA cannot fuse a scatter into its neighbours, and each bought its own kernel launch. At ≈1.6 ms per step that is ≈4 µs per dispatch — the launch constant and the op count turned out to be the same number seen twice. Rewriting positional writes as selects (the row index is arange(B), so the scatter is duplicate-free and a where is exactly equivalent and does fuse), passing statically known slot positions as Python integers rather than traced arrays, and removing floor_divide's sign-correction sequence in favour of shifts and lax.div with a CI-checked precondition, takes the step to 72 dispatches — scatter 168 → 0, gather 118 → 4, floor_divide 2,325 HLO instructions → 0.

The "before" figure has moved since the previous revision, and the reason is worth one sentence. It was 382 when this work began and is 404 now, because a mechanics change landed underneath the branch and added four more positional scatters per step. The hardened count did not move at all. That is the difference between a transformation that is structural and one that is a tuned constant: the port grew and the hardened step did not.

The regime shifted, and that is the finding rather than the peak. Reading ms/step down each column instead of across, the scanned path now grows very nearly linearly in batch from about B = 4,096; before, it did not start growing until B ≈ 16,000–65,536. The scanned simulator is device-bound at a quarter of the batch size it used to need — which is precisely the regime in which a bigger GPU is allowed to help, and it is what turns the re-probe below into a fair question rather than a foregone one. The per-step path remains host-bound below B ≈ 16,000 and always will be: what it pays now is the round-trip into the runtime, not the kernel, and no amount of op-count work removes a Python call.

A shape that did not exist before. The hardened port can draw its randomness and its actions inside the traced loop — sampling chance branches and drawing from the legal-action mask without a host round-trip between turns — where the previous port could not produce this measurement at all, because with no key carried in the state every turn of the scan redrew the same numbers. This is the honest end-to-end generation figure: 21.0M battle-turns/s at B = 16,000, rising to 26.9M at B = 65,536. It gives up ≈25% against the forced-branch row, and that gap is real work rather than overhead — a hash block per chance point plus a masked categorical per asked slot. It is the price of a rollout that is actually a rollout, and it is the number a training fleet would see.

And the H100 question is now open in a sharper form. The hardening raised the bar it must clear: because the consumer card's own baseline improved, an H100 must now reach 273.4M turns/s to justify its price, and the arithmetic predicts it would still miss by ≈4.3× — slightly worse than the 4.2× it was measured to miss by before, since the hardening helped the cheap card more. That remains a forecast. Unlike the previous revision, it is being tested: a re-probe of the hardened kernel on rented H100 silicon is running at the time of writing. pending The measured H100 re-probe against the hardened kernel.

What the same probe already found is that we were pointing it at the wrong workload. The H100 lost on the simulator because the simulator is a launch-bound integer kernel, which is close to the worst case for that card. On dense floating-point work — which is what a learner and an inference replica run, with no scatter and no integer chain — the same rented box measured 787 TFLOP/s sustained in bf16, and nothing in this campaign has priced that. So the architecture the numbers support is not "H100 or consumer card" but a split fleet: H100-class silicon for the learner and the inference replicas, consumer cards for game generation. That is also the ordering our own compute plan already had — actor/learner split, then GPU inference serving — arriving with a price attached rather than as a preference. It also relocates the re-probe that would actually change a decision: a learner and inference benchmark on that silicon, not a third simulator ladder.

8.16 First human opponents since the pivot

Every strength number in this paper is against Foul Play, which §1.2 adopted precisely because it predicts human performance better than the bots below it. But the ladder's honest rung is humans, and the Phase-1 system's record there — 1 win and 15 losses — is the gap this project exists to close. A tester seat now exists to re-measure it.

The seat. A network plays on a private Showdown server against invited human testers, from a pinned checkpoint snapshot rather than the live mirror: the week run rewrote its published weights continuously, so a seat reading the live file would have been a different opponent every half hour and its record would have meant nothing. The snapshot is taken by hash and frozen. The format is restricted to gen9 random battles as a hardcoded constant rather than a default — a challenge in any other format gets one polite refusal and zero battles started, verified live against a real gen9 OU challenge — because the network has never trained on any other format and a seat that quietly accepted one would generate exactly the kind of number this paper spends §6.4 retracting.

The first session: 2 wins, 3 losses against one tester. We are reporting a five-game record, so the caveats outweigh the result and are stated first. Five games resolves nothing: the 95% interval on 2/5 spans essentially the whole plausible range, one opponent is not a sample of humans, and a single tester's play style is a single style. The comparison figure needs its own correction too: the Phase-1 1W–15L is the natural-completion subset of a raw 7W–15L record, with four of the seven raw wins excluded as opponent forfeits inside ten turns. Both conventions matter, and the strict one is the one this paper has always quoted.

With those stated, the direction is what a reader would want to know: the previous system won one natural game in sixteen against human ladder opposition, and the current one won two of five against a single invited tester. Those are not the same experiment and the second is far too small to carry a comparison; the direction is consistent with everything in §8.4 and §8.12, and it is not evidence for anything on its own.

One provenance note, in the style of §7. Unlike every other result in this paper, this record is carried by an operator's session log rather than by a committed artifact we can re-derive from. The battle corpus lives on the evaluation box and is not yet in the repository. Under the campaign's own rule — any number quoted must re-derive from artifacts — that makes this the weakest-evidenced number in the paper, and it is flagged rather than dressed up. pending A committed human-eval corpus, and a session large enough to carry a claim.

8.17 The 8M arc, closed: a learning rate, a confirmation run, and a gate that failed

The previous revision left §10.2's first item as "the student is built and training." It finished, and then the reinforcement learning that was supposed to build on it destroyed it three times before we understood why. This section is that arc end to end. Its conclusion is a negative one, and it is the strongest empirical argument this campaign has produced for the hardware §10.3 wants to spend money on.

Act 1 — the seed. The distilled student is 8.13M parameters, fit to the banked 2.5M teacher's recomputed outputs, reaching teacher agreement 0.848 and — the number that matters — 44.3% [38.8, 50.0] over 300 games against full-strength Foul Play. That is statistically the teacher's equal at 3.3× the parameters, carrying the history seams the GPU phase needs. It was banked with checksums as the artifact of record, and it stayed the artifact of record through everything that follows.

Acts 2–4 — the wash, and two falsified explanations. Reinforcement learning from that seed did not improve it; it dismantled it. Arms fell 36.3 → 34.0 → 29.7% across iterations 94, 145 and 208 while a 2.5M control on the same fleet held 41.0%, and policy entropy climbed 0.64 → 1.02 — a policy diffusing rather than sharpening. Cutting the entropy coefficient 4× arrested the entropy climb and recovered no strength: falsified. Rolling all the way back to the seed (verified in weight space, not by filename) collapsed again at the same rate: the wash was a property of the training configuration, not of a corrupted checkpoint.

Act 5 was a misread, and the misread is the reportable part. The overnight record then logged a "clean learning-rate experiment" that ran the seed from step 0 at a changed rate, reproduced the identical pathology at iteration 70, and concluded that "LR is falsified too." It was not. That experiment moved the learning rate from 3e-4 to 1e-3 — upward — because the operator had read the rate off a preset block in a config file that the running job was not using. The one direction never tested was down. A correction was filed against our own overnight record, and a rule with it: never infer a running job's effective configuration by grepping presets — read the process's own config-override log line. That readback is now mandatory at every launch, with a literal string to match, and a "kill it" instruction if the string is absent.

The root cause, once the untested direction was tested. The 8.13M network had inherited the 2.5M's learning rate of 3e-4, which the same code had held healthy for 6,600 iterations — a free historical control nobody had thought to use as one. 8.18 An opponent with a compute budget is not a fixed bar

The gate above turns on a comparison between two win rates. Measuring it surfaced the fact that on a shared rig a win rate is not by itself a comparable quantity — and that finding generalises well past this campaign, so it is reported as a methods result rather than as an operations note.

The same checkpoint — same file, same sha256 — played the same opponent for the same 300 games twice in one day:

The mechanism is not subtle once stated. Foul Play is time-budgeted — it searches for a fixed wall-clock allowance per decision, not for a fixed node count. Contention steals that allowance, so the opponent searches less, plays weaker, and our number goes up by roughly ten points. Every strength number measured on a loaded shared box is partly a measurement of the box.

The direction is the dangerous one. Contention makes the system look better, and contention is what a busy campaign produces; the quiet box that gives the honest reading is the box you have when nothing else is running, which is to say when you are not making progress. An instrument that flatters you exactly when you are working hardest is worse than one that is merely noisy.

Three consequences, in ascending order of cost:

  1. No fixed percentage bar is valid on a shared rig. The 8M gate's original form — "some checkpoint ≥ 50% by iteration 300" — could have been passed or failed by another agent's grading job. It was rewritten before it was read, not after.
  2. The design that replaces it is a same-window anchor. Ship the reference checkpoint into the evaluation queue alongside the arm under test, under a second key, so the driver claims it as a separate arm inside the same load window. iter900 anchored the 150/200 window; iter901 — the same seed file, same hash, second key — anchored iter260's, because iter260 sat queued and would only start when a lane freed, in a later and therefore different window. Without the second anchor, the single arm the gate actually turned on would have been the one arm with no anchor.
  3. The infrastructure that removes the problem is separation of measurement compute from evaluation compute. Three dedicated 32-vCPU grading pods now run corpus grading — $0.96/hour each, $2.88/hour combined — so a grading job never again shares a box with an evaluation arm. Each pod had to reproduce the evaluation box's grade to the unit before being trusted: 1,238/1,841 = 67.2461% roll-conditioned, hpErrorAbs 26,110, 896 mismatches, every channel identical on all three pods and on the box they replace. A grading box that does not reproduce the box it replaces is a second instrument, not a copy.

One control was attempted and refused, and the refusal is the useful fact. Restoring parity rather than advantage — nice 0 for the entire evaluation rig, our seat and the Showdown server and Foul Play alike, so the matchup balance could not shift — failed with renice: Permission denied even as root, because the rented container drops cap_sys_nice. Scheduling priority is not available as an experimental control on rented infrastructure. Isolation is the only lever, which is why consequence 3 costs money.

The general rule: any evaluation against a compute-budgeted opponent must control for the opponent's compute. This is not a Pokémon fact. It applies to every benchmark whose reference player is a search engine with a time budget — which is most of them — and it is invisible to the code, to the test suite, and to the artifact re-derivation discipline of §7, because every number on both sides of the table above re-derives perfectly from its own committed artifacts. The rig was sound. The bar moved.

It also hands §8.8's longest-standing open question its first named candidate. Two evaluation rigs in this campaign disagree by a systematic +2.6pp (55 matched arms, paired p ≈ 0.0005) whose cause has been "unidentified" for three revisions. 8.20 One instrument, one field set, one population — and the comparison goes the other way

Every fork-versus-sim2 number this paper has printed compared two engines through two different graders: two corpora, two field sets, two conditioning rules, two denominators. §10.2's −25.23pp, round 6's crossover and round 6.1's un-crossing are all that comparison at different tips. Round 6.1 closed exactly one of the four confounds — the corpus draw, worth +0.2742pp to the fork — and then refused to subtract the two it could price, on the grounds that "subtracting them to get a single corrected number would be splicing two measurements taken under different instruments." Round 7 does the other thing: it removes the second instrument.

What that required, and it is three seams rather than a new grader. Neither engine's grader was touched. (1) A shim converts the schema-4 corpus of record to the schema-3 shape the fork's wire reads, which is lossless for a structural reason (the dump's record loop sits outside every doubles conditional) and is checked rather than argued: the shimmed corpus must equal an independently regenerated schema-3 dump at the same oracle, seed and count, field for field. It failed on its first attempt, on all 984 fixtures, on a header key written only on the doubles path — which is what the control is for. (2) A projection reimplements the grader's state diff clause for clause over the fork's canonical state, and a test perturbs every field of every record of every committed fixture and requires the two to produce the same path set, not merely the same verdict. That test caught a real divergence on its first run: read naively, the grader's boost guard treats a position whose occupant has fainted — still named by activeIndices until its replacement is chosen — as occupied, which charges stat stages on a corpse. (3) A ledger adapter wraps the fork's own adapter and, out of a single pass, answers three different questions per turn: the fork's stage-two pick, the branch-index pick (TIEBREAK=0 exactly), and does the engine's enumeration contain Showdown's answer on the shared fields — which is the same sentence sim2's metric already answers.

More than the headlines match: all fourteen of the fork's mismatch classes reproduce to the unit, which certifies in one line that the wrapper is transparent, the shim is lossless, and the regenerated corpus is the corpus of record.

8.21 The first resolved separation on the training side

The campaign has never had a strength comparison between two of its own networks that excluded zero. The 8M arc closed in §8.17 with a gate failure and a seed banked; the sharpest thing that could be said about the 8M against its own parent was −1.33pp [−7.29, +4.62] — an interval so wide it excluded nothing.

It is now resolved. The live 8M student at iteration 3,500 beats the banked 2.5M control 52.12% of games, 95% CI [51.64, 52.61], over 40,000 complete games in the harness's own real environment — +2.12pp over even, ≈ +14.8 Elo. The interval clears 50% by 1.6pp, more than three times its own half-width.

Two readings, both true, and they belong together. The 8M is not broken — against the best banked checkpoint of its parent run, the sign is no longer in doubt. And the flat value head is still the story: 3.3× the parameters and 2,000 iterations of live PPO bought about fifteen Elo over a network that had stopped improving before the 8M existed. The control is also not an equal-compute opponent, and the inequality runs against the 8M: it has trained 6,634 iterations and 558M samples to the subject's 3,500 and 296M. Read +2.12pp as "the 8M has passed the best banked 2.5M", not as a capacity result.

The measurement design is the transferable part, because it found a bias in the campaign's own tool. The head-to-head harness evaluates seat A's action before seat B's, so B's sampling stream depends on A's draws and the two orderings are not exact mirrors. An earlier lane measured a 49.13% null at n = 3,000 and called it the tool's resolution. It is not resolution — it is a bias, and it reproduces: four null runs, a byte-identical checkpoint against itself so the true rate is 50.00% by construction, pool to 49.27% [48.78, 49.76] over 40,000 games. The interval excludes 50. The A seat carries a −0.73pp handicap, and every A-seat-only number the tool has ever printed is depressed by about that much. The design here removes it rather than correcting for it — each net plays both roles for the same game count, so the order bias enters both halves equally and cancels — and three independent routes to the effect agree within ±0.1pp: role-averaged +2.12pp, A-seat minus A-seat null +2.22pp, B-seat minus B-seat null +2.03pp.

What the number is for. Underneath it, the learner's own metrics say the value head is stationary: value_loss has moved 0.5495 → 0.511 in 3,500 iterations and has sat inside ±0.01 of 0.505 for the last 2,500 of them, while the policy loss oscillates around zero at the third decimal. That is exactly the regime an MSE-on-a-sigmoid objective predicts and exactly what the BCE cut targets. So the run produced +2.12pp with its value head essentially stationary — which is the most useful single sentence for deciding what the pending fixes are worth. Two of them are already live-cut into the running learner (an entropy controller and a loss-rebalance annealer, the latter actively decaying the opponent-head coefficient to about ×0.63 of its start, with the gradient share now roughly 75% policy); the BCE value objective is the third. The document that carries this baseline is written as a re-run script rather than as a result: same frozen control checkpoint by md5, same seeds, same game count, same environment, same eight-run matrix, so that the next reading is comparable rather than merely later. Two non-overlapping intervals will be a real move; anything under ~1pp will not be. The whole baseline cost $0.43 on a rented 4090, and the live learner was never computed on — two checksum reads, two file copies and one read of a metrics file.

8.22 The re-measurement, and a loss curve that was shrinking its own objective

§8.21 was written as a re-run script rather than as a result, on the explicit promise that the next reading would be comparable rather than merely later. The trigger condition was three live cuts acting on the learner — an entropy controller, a loss-rebalance annealer, and the trunk released as both auxiliary heads reached their minimum scale. It fired at iteration ≈4,200, roughly 700 iterations past the baseline. The re-run went the wrong way, and this section is what a measurement is for.

The same 8M line, now annealed, against the same frozen 2.5M control identified by the same md5, under the same eight-run role-averaged matrix and the same game count, scores 50.54% [50.05, 51.03] against the baseline's 52.12% [51.64, 52.61], both over 40,000 games. The intervals are disjoint. In Elo the run went from +14.8 to +3.7 over its own control: the move is −1.58pp, ≈ −11 Elo, across the 700 iterations that span the entropy controller, the annealer and the entropy walk that followed them.

The instrument is beyond reproach on this one, which is the only comfortable sentence here. The null runs — a byte-identical checkpoint against itself, true rate 50.00% by construction — reproduced the baseline's nulls run for run: 49.13% against 49.13%, 49.46% against 49.46%. Not "within noise" — the same numbers. The §8.21 A-seat bias reproduced with them. Whatever moved, it was not the ruler. The whole re-run cost $0.14.

And the learner's own metrics say what moved, in a way that is more interesting than the sign. Across iterations 3,525 → 4,199, training loss fell 0.7142 → 0.2864, which every dashboard in this campaign reads as learning. It is not learning: it is the objective shrinking. The annealer floors both auxiliary coefficients at ×0.050, which removes most of the loss's magnitude without adding a single point of policy quality — and the component that would show quality moved the other way. value_loss went 0.5112 → 0.5142, worse by 0.003; the plateau §8.21 named is untouched by all three cuts aimed near it. policy_loss moved −0.0037 → −0.00039, i.e. it oscillates around zero at the fourth decimal. The opponent-action auxiliary head sits at aux_opp_action_acc 0.5019, which the learner's own report reads as chance — vindicating the decision to floor it, and saying that the capacity those parameters represent has not been converted into anything. Calibration, by contrast, is genuinely good and stayed good (calib_ece 0.0081, calib_out_ece 0.0102, fitted temperature 1.07), so the §8.5 calibration defect that motivated the BCE cut is no longer the visible bottleneck even though the value head is still flat. Entropy sat at 0.7595 → 0.7735 — the controller is holding its setpoint, and holding it is not the same as the setpoint being right.

What this does and does not license. It is a confounded −1.58pp: 700 ordinary iterations, an entropy controller, an annealer and an entropy walk all changed together, and the design measures only their net effect. No single cut is convicted by it. The decision taken was therefore to hold the cuts rather than roll them back, and to re-measure at ≈iteration 5,500 — the point at which the annealed configuration will have had as many iterations to convert as the baseline had — with the explicit rule that if the number is still shrinking, the rollback package goes to the operator with both readings attached rather than one.

Two things about it belong in a paper rather than a changelog. The first is that this is the measurement working: three interventions were cut into a live run on plausible mechanistic grounds, a pre-existing baseline was written specifically so that they could be scored, and the score came back negative and disjoint within a day. The alternative — the one this campaign ran on for its first six revisions — is a falling loss curve and a dashboard. The second is procedural and cost nothing to learn but would have cost a result: latest.pt rewrites every ≈7.7 minutes, so a checkpoint copied by path is not a checkpoint identified by hash. The copy is now bracketed — md5, copy, md5, five ways — rather than assumed stable across reads.

8.23 Foul Play, on a clean read: 3.2× the parameters buys nothing

The other half of the same question was asked against the external opponent, and the answer is a null that matters more than the §8.2 null did, because it is measured under constant conditions on a rig built for exactly that purpose (§8.18's dedicated grading pods).

On an uncontended pod, the annealed 8M at checkpoint 4,250 scores 40.17% [37.53, 42.86] over n = 1,297 games against full-strength Foul Play, against a matched 2.5M control's 41.64% in the same window. p = 0.34. They are the same number. Taken with §8.22's h2h edge — which the same window shrinks to +0.54pp — the reading is blunt: 3.2× the parameters is buying approximately nothing in win rate at this scale of experience, post-cuts.

An earlier, larger number is retracted here rather than quietly dropped. A matched same-box window had read the 8M at 44.60% [42.83, 46.38] against the control's 41.64% [40.10, 43.20] — +2.96pp, with the trend rising. It did not survive newer checkpoints: on the matched key the difference is now +1.10pp at p = 0.39. The +2.96pp was never wrong arithmetic; it was a slice.

Two instrument corrections came out of the lane and both run against previous claims of ours. The first is a downward revision of our own worst-case caveat: this paper has carried, since the evaluation-rewire work, an assertion that one evaluation box's absolute win rates are inflated by ≈10pp under serving load. Measured, it is 1.5pp. That supersedes the ≈10pp figure and makes cross-era comparisons healthier than the paper has been treating them — a correction that runs in the direction of our own numbers being better than claimed, which is exactly why it is stated explicitly rather than absorbed. The second is a data-quality check that came back clean: Foul Play occasionally wins by disconnect-forfeit on battles whose wall clock exceeds 300 s, which is a one-directional upward bias, and the driver was found not to filter them (it never reads wall_s; its ceiling was unapplied). The bias was then measured rather than assumed, and it is symmetric and tiny — +0.119pp on the subject arm, +0.190pp on the control, +0.146pp on the week run — so nothing this paper reports moves. The filter is applied from here on and the ceiling is wired; the honest note is that it was measured after being discovered, not before.

The clean read cost ≈$1.55. Its function in the argument is to remove the last comfortable explanation for §8.22: the training-side separation shrank, and the external-opponent separation was never there to shrink. What is left is the training-scale thesis — that the recipe is stable and starved, and that the experiment which separates "stable and slowly decaying" from "stable and starved" is a run at a game-generation rate this campaign has not yet had. §8.24 onward is the machinery for that run.

Equivalence, and the verification is the part worth reading. The device encoder is compared against the host one over 12,003 real simulator states on the lead lane's rig and 12,007 rows in six shards on the verifier's independently regenerated corpus. Token ids, attention masks and legality bits are bit-exact — zero mismatches. Float channels are bounded at 1 ULP universally, on a bound that is derived rather than fitted (the reduction error is (N−1) ULP by argument, not by tuning): 266 of 269 channels are bit-identical, all 269 agree within 2 × 10⁻⁶, and the worst channel, exp_damage, diverges by 4.77 × 10⁻⁷. The largest ULP usage anywhere in the suite is a matchup channel at 8.0 ULP, which is difference-cancellation between large similar quantities and is disclosed as an absolute-tolerance case rather than folded into the headline. The divergences are the ones XLA's reciprocal-multiply lowering predicts, and the same mechanism appeared between the module's own JIT and eager paths in CI — which is how we know it is a lowering property and not a port defect.

Three honest limits attach to that corpus and are stated rather than smoothed. 13 of 21 diversity axes are simulator-unreachable today — weather, terrain, boosts and status live in handler lanes that were mid-merge (§8.25) — so 75% of corpus rows carry injected well-formed state rather than simulated state. That is legitimate for a pure state function and illegitimate as evidence about the simulator, and the two claims are kept apart. Two observation fields (item_known, ability_known) were found to be false on this path, an implementation defect that was invisible precisely because both encoders agreed on it — an equivalence test certifies sameness, never correctness. And the corpus half of the suite is not run in CI; it is a pod run, gated by hand.

The verification story is §7's seventh class in miniature: the rig's knife-edge amnesty was too permissive and was caught by an injected >= → > mutation that it had silently excused 82,740 times; the comparator was blind to NaN and Inf and reported a fully poisoned channel as clean; both were fixed and both fixes were re-proved by re-injecting the same mutations and requiring a non-zero exit with named cells.

The next sized item, because it is already measured. With encoding off the critical path, the dominant remaining host cost is the environment's own device→host copy: env.step pulls ≈80 arrays back per step when the loop reads ≈9 of them, and that pull is 48.9–73.8% of env.step (73.8% at 512 environments, 52.6% at 1,024, 52.8% at 2,048, 48.9% at 4,096) against 6–7% for the simulator kernel itself. Restricting it to the nine keys measures at 0.2–0.3% of the step — a 170–250× reduction on that term, and the largest single item left on the throughput list. It was deliberately not taken inside this lane: different file, different verification surface. The first attempt to size it was 40× wrong because the harness's own sampling contaminated the timing, which is why the corrected figure is quoted as a range rather than a point.

And the scaling arithmetic, which is an extrapolation and is labelled as one. The only comparison the lane itself would sign is a yardstick, not a projection: the Jaxcalibur recipe generates 100M games in one H100-week, i.e. 165.3 games/s per card. Before this lane, the JAX actor ran at 11.5 games/s — 14× short. After it, one consumer 4090 runs at 97.4 — 1.7× short. That is the whole of what is measured.

The sizing answer the campaign is planning against goes one step further and is stated separately because it is ours, not the lane's. One 4090 is memory-capped, not compute-capped: 4,096 environments with the network in the loop exhausts 24 GB. On 80 GB cards the same loop holds a much larger batch, and the naive projection is ≈150–250 battles/s per card ⇒ ≈600–1,000 battles/s across four ⇒ ≈50–85M battles/day, which would put Jaxcalibur's 100M-game corpus at 1.5–2 days of wall clock rather than the months of CPU generation §8.17 measured as the binding constraint.

The lane lead explicitly refused to publish that multiplication, and the refusal is the more useful artifact. Its four reasons: the throughput harness is lighter than a real actor; H100 scaling across the memory boundary is unmeasured; the physics is incomplete — 81 handler registrations are not yet executed and ≈27% of move slots are inert, and battles get longer as they land, which lowers games/s; and _pull is still there, which moves the number the other way. Two of those push the estimate down and one pushes it up, and the campaign's own history on exactly this inference is bad: §8.15 predicted an H100 win and measured an H100 loss for $3.17. So the projection is recorded here as the sizing hypothesis §8.27's run exists to test, with the same standing rule §8.15 wrote: it will be replaced by a measurement or retracted, and no rental decision is taken on it alone.

The measurement arrived the next day and the projection was wrong by an order of magnitude (§8.29). Three actors on three H100s measured ≈17 battles/s per card, not 150–250, because the premise the projection rested on — that the loop is memory-capped and 80 GB buys the batch to fill it — is false: the actor loop is host-bound, at 1% GPU utilisation on a card ten times larger, and a host-bound loop does not get faster when you hand it more device memory. The _pull fix the projection assumed also arrived, and it is worth 1.54–1.75× on the step rather than the 170–250× on the pull segment this section quoted (§8.32 (i)). The lane's refusal to publish the multiplication was correct, and the campaign's record on cross-device projection is now 0 for 2.

8.27 gpu-q1: a quarantined lineage, and the honest banner it ships under

On 2026-09-04 the GPU lineage this whole wave was built for started training. It is a fresh 8.19M-parameter network (8,190,204 parameters) on the 89-wide action space, generated by the JAX environment with the device-side encoder of §8.26, entropy-controlled from iteration zero at a target of 0.75, with the auxiliary annealer off because the run is fresh rather than warm-started.

Why it is a new lineage and not a cutover, which is a measurement rather than a preference. The plan of record had been to switch the existing fleet onto the GPU actor once its parity gate went green. A dedicated lane measured that plan and reversed it, on two independent grounds. It is impossible by construction: the GPU lineage is 89-wide under one observation encoding and the live fleet is 14-wide under another, so the learner refuses the other side's shards — measured directly, fresh = 3 / layout = 0 at 89 against fresh = 0 / layout = 3 at 14. A mixed fleet cannot exist, which makes the "cutover" a training restart on a new lineage however it is described. And it was, at that moment, undesirable: the GPU actor ran 11.5–11.9 battles/s flat across a 4× change in batch, at 0% GPU utilisation, while a CPU actor on the same pod did ≥17.85 — the GPU path was ≈35% slower, and the host-side numpy encoder was the reason. §8.26 is the lane that came out of that finding. The recommendation the measurement produced — a quarantined 89-wide lineage running beside an untouched CPU fleet — is the shape this run actually has. The standing "cut over when the gate goes green" rule is amended in the same breath: a green gate remains a correctness milestone, but its premise that the GPU path is faster was measured false and the speed claim now has to be made separately.

The dry run first, because the launch was gated on it. Fifty iterations end to end on the real tree: loss 0.9176 → 0.4684, value_loss 0.05249 → 0.00157, entropy 2.0314 → 1.3561 against the 0.75 setpoint with the controller's coefficient walking 0.01000 → 0.00091, weights_version 0 → 10 with 4 swaps and 0 rejections — i.e. the weight round-trip from learner to actor closes. The three quarantine counters — layout mismatch, encoding mismatch, quarantined rows — were zero on every iteration, which is the property that says the 89-wide shards and the 14-wide fleet cannot touch each other. It also found one real defect: a standing feed_weights_absent of 11, shards published before the first weight version existed and therefore never accepted, leaving a one-time 5.91 GB disk puddle. The reclaimer was wrapped around it; at the live launch the first shards already carried a weight version and the puddle did not recur.

Live metrics, at iteration 4. Loss 0.624; entropy 1.944 walking down toward the setpoint with the coefficient driving at 0.0078; 2,316 samples/s; zero feed waits; weights_version 1 with swaps accepted and 0 rejected; all three quarantine counters zero. The actor produced 583 games and 131k rows in its first 90 seconds, generation running ahead of consumption. The launch shape is n_envs 512 on the actor and 128 on the learner — the batch already proven safe rather than the fastest one; §8.26 measures the knee at 2,048, and moving there is a tuning step gated on stability, not a launch decision.

The banner this run ships under, and it travels with every number it produces. gpu-q1 is a full-information lineage: the reveal-mask property of §5.3 — machine-checked on the CPU environment, and the reason any of this campaign's transfer numbers mean anything — is not yet staffed on the JAX side. Both seats see the whole state. The run's own launch document states it in one sentence and this paper adopts it verbatim: "a network trained here reads facts a player cannot and will not transfer." That is a legitimate configuration for measuring throughput, stability, entropy control and learner mechanics, and it is not one whose win rates transfer to a ladder or to any opponent that hides information. No strength figure from this lineage is comparable to §8.4's, §8.12's or §8.21's until the masks land. We print it at launch rather than at publication because the failure mode this paper most wants to avoid is a beautiful curve trained on information a real opponent would hide — §5.3's opening sentence, now with a run temporarily on the wrong side of it.

Symmetrically, 21 GB that looked irreplaceable was proved byte-identical to an existing mirror and deliberately not copied.

Two procedural findings from the teardown generalise past this campaign. A process-count check taken seconds after a kill does not prove a fleet is down. The first stop pass reported clean; the md5 re-verification minutes later caught two files still changing, because two supervisors had respawned their children and one of them ignored the termination signal. Only the checksum re-check saw it — the same shape as §7's fifth class, one layer up: the thing that was wrong was the confirmation, not the action. And the learner honoured a single termination signal at its next iteration boundary with one final checkpoint, exiting in 19 s; a second signal would have been a hard exit and was deliberately not sent. A system that recycles its own experience (§10.1) needs an explicit bank step and an explicit quiesce step, and this is the second time the campaign has learned that at shutdown.

What is frozen, and what it is now for. The 8M is pinned as an immutable anchor at iteration 5,225, identified by md5, read-only and verified untouched through the teardown. It is the fixed opponent that every GPU-lineage strength number in §8.30 is measured against. The era that produced it is closed; the artefact it produced is the ruler.

8.29 Four H100s, day one: the learner was the wall

The hardware the whole GPU wave was built to justify went live on 2026-09-04: four H100 80GB SXM cards on one rented host, 224 vCPU, at $13.96/hour. One card runs the learner, three run actors. The actors, measured rather than projected. Three actors on three H100s produced 50.24 battles/s aggregate (17.28 / 15.98 / 16.98 per card) at 10,922.6 rows/s. This is where §8.26's projection meets its measurement, and the projection loses. §8.26 recorded ≈150–250 battles/s per card ⇒ ≈600–1,000 across four as a sizing hypothesis, on the explicit premise that one consumer card is memory-capped and 80 GB would buy the batch to fill it. The premise was wrong in a way the measurement makes obvious: a host-bound loop does not get faster when you give it more device memory. Measured day one is ≈17 battles/s per card against a projection of 150–250 — an order of magnitude, in the direction §8.15's precedent should have warned us about. Two related corrections belong beside it, both from the controlled same-box comparison rather than a solo benchmark: the GPU actor path measures 11.46–11.93 battles/s flat across a 4× batch range — flatness across batch being the signature of a dispatch-and-host cost rather than a compute one — and an earlier 1.73× rows-per-battle inflation factor, derived from mismatched fleet ratios, is corrected to a measured 1.21×.

The units trap, which is why "battles per day" comparisons in this domain mislead. Three distinct denominators were confused on this box in one day, and each of them flatters the GPU arm. Rows are not games: rows accrue for every in-flight battle while games count only completions, so a larger in-flight population charges more unfinished rows against the same battle budget — a rows/s comparison between two arms at different environment counts is not a comparison. Samples per second is not samples per second across feed shapes, which is the +43%-versus-+20% lesson above. And most importantly, generated is not consumed: 62% of day one's generation was discarded stale, so a headline "battles generated" figure overstates the training the run actually received by a factor of nearly three. The dashboard was changed to carry generated and trained-on as two separate tiles for exactly this reason, and the run's own manifest — not a file count — was made the authority for both (§8.32 (ii)). This paper therefore quotes the measured rate and the consumed fraction and declines to print a day-one battle total, because the total that circulated was a double-count and the corrected one lives on the run rather than in the repository.

The disk, three times, and the self-healing that came out of it. Discarded shards accumulate, and on day one the box came within about eleven minutes of a full disk. The first reclaim freed 176.30 GB across 82 files, taking the volume from 246 GB used to 29 GB used, and a periodic reclaimer was installed behind it. The second and third incidents are the interesting ones, because the watchdog reported 0.00 GB freed on every sweep while the disk fell from 494 GB free to 53 GB — and it was right both times. Staleness is measured against the learner's published weights version, so a learner that is stopped or hung freezes the clock the reclaimer reads, and nothing ever becomes stale. The third occurrence generalised it: a backlog of 220 shards and 474 GB sat entirely inside the freshness window, because staleness bounds a shard's age and never the queue's length. Both are now documented in the watchdog's own header — the right place for a correct-but-surprising behaviour — and the fix is a hard backlog cap alongside the age rule. The watchdog itself was hardened into something that survives its own operator: a pid file, an exclusive lock, and a keepalive observed live catching a dead loop and restarting it. One operational scar is recorded with it: a pattern-matched kill of the reclaimer's own name terminated the operator's session, the command line having matched itself.

What the learner learned, which is the reason any of this matters. Against a random opponent the fresh network went from 49.6% (254/512, i.e. chance) to 99.41% (509/512) in 49 iterations, with loss 0.624 → 0.442 and entropy walking 2.03 → 1.42 against its 0.75 setpoint as the controller drove its coefficient 0.0100 → 0.00065. At the day's handoff the run sat at iteration 137, loss 0.3678, weights version 52, feed waits flat, all three quarantine counters still zero. Beating random is a floor and not a result — §8.30 is the measurement that counts — but it is the floor that says the pipeline end-to-end is intact on new hardware.

And one defect is left open rather than closed with a story. The league evaluation hung the learner for over twenty minutes, twice, around iteration 99. A root cause was published — a frozen-self match in the evaluation path — and the fix, cutting the evaluation to 128 games, brought it to 36.6 s. Then it recurred at iteration 299, hanging ≈30 minutes with 72 shards queued behind it, and the published root cause was retracted: every league row on the run records a random opponent, so the frozen-self match the explanation depended on had never run at all. The current suspicion is per-pass environment construction inside the evaluation path. It is recorded here as unestablished, because a retracted root cause with a plausible replacement is exactly the shape of thing this paper has learned to stop calling a fix.

One install trap is worth a sentence because it cost preflight time and names nothing useful: the pinned framework version pulls a compiler wheel that floats to a newer release, and that wheel ships as a namespace package whose file attribute is None, so the import dies with a type error mentioning neither the framework nor CUDA. The pin belongs on the compiler wheel, not the framework.

8.30 A strength ladder on real Showdown, against a pinned anchor

Every GPU-lineage number this paper has printed so far is a sim number: throughput, stability, entropy, agreement against an oracle. §8.27's banner says why that is not strength — the lineage trains on full information, and a network that reads facts a player cannot is not a ladder policy. This section is the instrument that turns the banner into a measurement rather than a disclaimer.

The adapter, and the constraint it was built under. A policy adapter plugs an 89-wide GPU checkpoint into a live Pokémon Showdown server through poke-env: it implements the same policy interface the trainer uses and subclasses the existing transfer seat. Not one line of an existing file changed — that was a design constraint, not an accident, and the reason is §6.1's: the shared files it reuses are exactly the files whose tests certify the training/serving seam, so every edit to one would have been an edit to the thing those tests certify. The single hook the adapter needed — lifting the opponent's protocol action into the wide space before the history deriver runs — was taken at an existing subclass override point rather than by widening a shared method. Five new files, sixteen new tests.

The 14 → 89 action-image lift, and the two things the narrow space could not say. The transfer harness had always spoken to Showdown through a 14-wide action image. 8.31 The reveal mask, built against the fork cell for cell — and the recipe it refused

§5.3 is this paper's claim that hidden information is machine-checked rather than assumed. §8.27 is a run temporarily on the wrong side of it. This section is the arm that closes the gap, and it is the clearest verification exhibit of the wave — including in the part where it declines to fix something.

What it is. The Rust reveal tracker of §5.3 — the thing that decides what each seat has legitimately observed — ported to the GPU stack as a pure function over two state pytrees, filling all eleven observation planes that the schema had declared since it was written and that the GPU environment had never once populated. Alongside it, a masked arm in the token encoder applying the CPU chain's masked-view semantics.

The placement is the correctness argument, and it is worth stating as a general rule. The mask is applied to the projection, not to the finished token tensor: a seat-to-seat function inserted between projection and encoding, mirroring the numpy chain where the masked view is taken first and everything downstream runs unchanged. The reason is that every derived channel — the damage chain, the matchup grid, hits-to-KO, the effective-stat estimator — is computed from facts, and masking the tensor afterwards would leave the opponent's best-damage estimate computed from their real stats, real item and unrevealed moves. That is a leak with no symptom, in the channels a policy leans on hardest. Mask the inputs, not the outputs.

The encoding id is made distinct by construction rather than by hand. Masking is one of six facts hashed into the shard's encoding id, so flipping the arm moves the digest automatically: full-information reports spenc-v1+7ede8d57, masked reports spenc-v1+686b58e0, and a test asserts the other five facts do not budge when the arm flips. This matters because the campaign has been bitten by the opposite: an id that fails to move when the encoding does lets two incompatible lineages pool as if comparable, which is the defect class §10.4 records under a different name.

The evidence. Against the production numpy masked chain, 144 rows on the tracked-planes arm and 144 rows on a seeded random-planes arm, with zero mismatches and zero excused cells on both — reproduced on three stacks: a CPU workstation, a rented consumer GPU running the training lineage's own pinned library set, and CI on every push. Nine cell-by-cell non-leakage claims each assert the viewer's ids, floats and masks are cell-identical while the owner's own view changes — the two-sided form, because a mask that blanks both seats passes a one-sided test trivially. Twelve injected mutants are caught by name (eight of nine in the encoder suite, four of four in the fold suite).

The thirteenth mutant survived, and it is reported as a finding rather than patched away. A mutation to the PP gate did not fail any test — because in this observation layout an opponent's PP reaches no channel at all: move tokens are built from the viewer's own active moves plus Struggle, and the mon block carries no PP column. The gate is defensive, not load-bearing. Rather than delete it or excuse it, the lane wrote a test proving that opening the gate changes the tensor by nothing, so the day a future layout gives PP a channel, that test goes red and the mutant rejoins the list. That is the §7-seventh-class discipline applied to its own result: a test that passes because it cannot fail is a finding, and the honest response is to make its inertness explicit and expiring.

The recipe it refused, which is the exhibit. Wiring the masked encoder into the device path had two candidate recipes, and the shorter one leaks. The rejected option overrides the masked flag on the device encoder's own report — fewer files touched, and wrong, because the mask is enforced in two places that read the same array: the token encoder enforces it inside the 60 tokens, and the event deriver enforces it inside the 16 history rows. Overriding only the encoder's report would have produced shards whose tokens are masked, whose history rows carry the opponent's exact HP fraction, and whose manifest declares masked: true — a leak shipped under a masked banner, which is strictly worse than the unmasked run it replaces because the unmasked run is labelled. The accepted recipe instead exposes the observation planes on the environment itself, so both paths read the same source and neither can reach around it. A third apparent option — passing the flag through explicitly — is not one: it skips the probe and the history deriver then fails on its first call.

And one live defect it found in code it did not own, deliberately reproduced rather than fixed. The CPU fork's own masked encoder indexes [:, US, 0] where it means [:, US, :, 0]: shaped (battle, side, party slot, stat), that reads party slot 0's six stats instead of six slots' max HP, so every bench slot's own HP percentage is computed against the wrong denominator — slot 1's HP bar divided by slot 0's attack, slot 2's by slot 0's defence. Slot 0 is right by coincidence. It reaches the mon block's HP fraction, the matchup block's own-HP channel and the field block's team-HP channel, and it is live in the CPU lineage's masked encoder today. A mon at 213/253 reads 84% where the intended index gives 85%.

The port reproduces the bug on purpose, with the reasoning written into the code and a test that asserts the defect is still present upstream — so the day the fork is corrected, this test goes red rather than the two encoders silently disagreeing. The argument for not simply fixing it is the one this campaign keeps re-learning: fixing it on one side only would leave two encoders with the same layout id, the same six encoding facts and therefore the same encoding id, producing numerically different observations that the quarantine would happily pool. Fixing it on the fork side moves the CPU lineage's encoding id and quarantines every shard it has in flight. Either way the fix is a coordinated change with a retraining decision attached, and it is filed as one rather than smuggled in as a cleanup.

A sourcing note this paper owes its own standard. A separate adversarial verifier was commissioned for this arm and the coordinator's log records it as having contributed the integration correction above. When the paper went to re-derive that claim, no verifier artefact could be found in the repository on any branch — every figure in this section re-derives from the lane's own committed report and artefacts instead, and the two-recipe analysis is reported here as the lane's self-verification because that is what can be sourced. The verifier's separate mutant battery is therefore not claimed. This is §7's standing amendment — a lane must commit its artefact directory, not merely quote headline numbers — collecting a second scalp, this time from the coordinator rather than a lane.

The arm was built and held at a clean seam: verified, in CI, and deliberately not wired into the running lineage, because it lands at the next lineage restart by design.

That restart happened the following night, and three statements above are corrected by it rather than edited out (§8.33). First, the arm is no longer held: it is the live lineage, and §8.27's full-information banner no longer governs any running number. Second, the encoding ids printed above have moved. They were the lane's own, hashed over six facts; shipping the arm added a seventh (an encoder-arithmetic version, with a legacy escape so that a report predating the fact digests identically), and the three arms that actually exist are spenc-v1+071785f2 (full-information, deliberately unmoved so no historical shard is re-quarantined), spenc-v1+b5b139a4 (masked, legacy HP arithmetic) and spenc-v1+bd3d8bca (masked, fixed HP arithmetic). The property the section claims — that the id moves by construction when the arm does — is what held; the specific digests were a snapshot of a pre-integration fact set. Third, the fork indexing defect this arm deliberately reproduced was fixed rather than carried, because the restart made it free: the objection to fixing it was that it moves an encoding id and quarantines in-flight shards, and a fresh lineage has none. The fix and the id move shipped as the one change this section argued they had to be — and §8.34 records that it reached one of the two encoder paths on the first attempt.

What the five share. None was caught by a test, because in every case the code was right and the number, the label or the attribution was wrong: a benchmark measuring a cache, a dashboard counting files, a format inferred from a width, a defect diagnosed against a stale tree, and a census row naming the wrong owner. §7's classes five through seven say to suspect the instrument, the machine and the verifier; this day's yield says to add a fourth — suspect the reading. The countermeasure that worked in every one of the five was the same and is cheap: make the claim re-derive from a committed artefact, and let the person who owns the code the claim is about be the one who re-derives it.

8.33 The lineage ruled poison, and the graph wiped by choice

§8.27 launched the GPU lineage under an explicit banner: it trains on full information, so "a network trained here reads facts a player cannot and will not transfer." §8.30 built the instrument that turns that banner into a measurement and the measurement was bad. This section is what was done about it, and what was done about it was to throw the lineage away.

The ruling. The owner's instruction was two sentences and it settled a question this paper had been carrying as a caveat for two rounds: "it needs to not cheat… wipe all the info on the graph, it's poison." The full-information rows were archived aside and removed from the live dashboard — not because any of them was measured wrong, but because a strength curve nobody may cite is a curve that will eventually be cited, and a footnote is not a load-bearing structure. §6.4 records the withdrawal, including the number it cost: 18.67% [13.24, 25.66] over 150 games against the pinned anchor, reached in a single evening from §8.30's 7.33%, and the best figure this campaign has ever produced against it. The honest statement is that we do not know what that lineage was worth and now never will.

Two successors in one night, and the reason there are two. The night ran roughly four and a half hours from the stop order to a stable masked run, and it produced two lineages rather than one:

The decision put to the owner was framed as a bundle: fix them now at the cost of the hours already spent, or carry them for the life of the lineage. The ruling was pay the hours now, and the discipline it encodes is worth naming, because it inverts the usual instinct: a restart is cheap exactly while a run is young, so the correct time to be maximally fussy about inputs is the first hours, not the first weeks. Masking is free, which was not obvious in advance. The live masked run holds 4,858 samples/s against the full-information run's 4,921 at the same batch shape and topology — a difference inside the noise of the measurement, and certainly not a cost worth trading correctness for. The reason is §8.31's placement argument: the mask is a pure function over two state pytrees applied at projection time, inside the same compiled step, so it adds arithmetic to a kernel rather than a host round trip. A masking scheme implemented after the token tensor — which is the obvious way, and the leaky way — would also have been the slow way.

Ignition ran behind four fail-closed gates. Before the first weight update the launcher asserts, and refuses to start on any failure, that the encoder reports the masked arm, that the encoding id is not the full-information one, that the observation planes are actually populated, and — a fourth added the same night by the fix in §8.34(v) — that the own-side HP bar uses the corrected denominator. The gates were validated the only way a gate can be: they were run against the live full-information encoder, and all of them refused it. §7's ninth class is the generalisation.

Two things were deliberately not fixed, and both are recorded because ignition was the free moment for them. Volatiles are not encoded on any channel — not Substitute, Leech Seed, confusion, Taunt, Encore, choice lock, a Protect counter, Wish, or the last move used — although the simulator publishes all of them. That is the lineage's standing observation scope, shared with the frozen 8M anchor, and it is layout-shaped: adding a volatile channel later changes the layout id and forces exactly the restart this audit existed to prevent. It was left as an owner decision rather than smuggled in. And our own side's HP is quantised to whole percent and rounded up, which is lossier than the game is to a real player, who reads exact HP out of the request. That is documented, symmetric across both sides by design, bounded at ≈1%, and — like the volatiles — cheaper to fix now than ever again. Both are on the record as choices with a price rather than as oversights.

What the cutover cost, stated plainly. The campaign's GPU strength graph restarts at zero. There is no masked strength number in this paper, the ladder of §8.30 has no rungs on the current lineage, and the honest summary of the round's results column is empty. Slower and honest is the trade that was made, and the reason it is in a results paper at all is that the alternative — a curve climbing under a banner explaining that it does not mean anything — is the failure mode this document exists to avoid.

8.34 The verification ledger: five defects, five different mechanisms

The night's five defects are collected here rather than distributed to their sites, because the distribution is the finding. Each was invisible to a green build. Each was caught by a different layer of the protocol in §7, and no two by the same one — which is the strongest available evidence that the layers are not redundant with each other.

1 and 2: the defects only a real construction can see. The layout comparison had survived two merges and a dedicated verifier. It could not have been found by any of them, because every test that exercised the path built the encoder against a stub, and a stub cannot have a type mismatch with a real layout object. The observed omission is the sharper of the two: the batched environment's default is full information, so a construction that forgets the argument produces a working, fast, plausible run that is simply not masked. Without the gates, the night's entire purpose would have been silently defeated on the first launch, and the tell would have been a strength curve that looked too good — which this campaign has now learned is not a tell anybody notices in time. §7's ninth class is written from these two.

3: a safety argument that was false, found by attacking it. The data-parallel learner (§10.2) claims that N ranks hold disjoint shard claims and that a rank is only swept when it is genuinely dead. Its heartbeat file was written by opening it for truncation and then writing — so between those two operations the file is empty, a reader parses nothing, the parse failure is handled as "infinitely stale", and a peer sweeps a rank that is alive and mid-poll. The verifier reproduced it twice: deterministically, and then with two real threads, where 233 of 530 reads of a continuously alive rank's heartbeat returned infinity. The duty cycle was measured (median 7.8 × 10⁻⁵ of a poll interval, from 2,000 timed writes) and converted into a rate — roughly one false sweep per rank pair per day at two ranks — which is what turned it from a race into a launch blocker. The worst of its three consequences is that a shard can be trained twice, which is the exact property the mechanism asserts cannot happen. Three artefacts stated the false argument in prose, and two passing tests encoded it; a suite that certifies a wrong safety model is worse than one that is silent about it. The fix is one atomic rename. What is worth carrying is the verifier's own diagnosis of why its mutation battery had missed it: of fifteen seeded defects the suite caught nine, and the one it could not see was the defect itself — nobody had written a test that reads the heartbeat while a write is in flight. The verdict was FAIL; the fixes landed, the regression test was added, the bit-identity of the single-rank path was re-checked against main, and it merged. That FAIL → fix → PASS cycle is the protocol working as designed, and it is reported at full length because the alternative reading — "the verifier found nothing" — is what a suppressed verdict looks like.

4: the leak the owner's bedtime question found. With the masked lineage already running, the owner asked whether anything else would poison the model forever if it were not fixed now. That question commissioned a pre-ignition audit of every model input and every training target, and it returned one launch-gating finding. The 68-channel history block computes four HP-derived channels; one of them uses the masked percentage bar and three use the raw fraction. Both lines were written in the same commit, by the same author, with the module's own docstring promising the mask for "the opponent's HP channels", plural — so this is not a masking pass that arrived later and missed a channel, it is a module recording its own inconsistency and shipping it.

The magnitude was priced before the fix was sized, and it is smaller than the words "full information leak" suggest — which is why it is worth reporting precisely. An unrevealed opposing Pokémon has never been on the field, so it reads full HP either way and contributes identically; what leaks is exact-versus-rounded HP on the revealed slots, under one percent per Pokémon. The sharper edge is the second channel: an exact damage fraction, integrated over a battle, reconstructs the opponent's real maximum HP and therefore its defensive spread, which is precisely the quantity the observation is designed to withhold. And the sharpest case is one the audit flagged as unknown rather than asserting: if the reveal tracker latches an opponent's HP bar at the value last seen on the field, then a Pokémon that switches out and heals — Regenerator, Leftovers on the bench, a Wish landing on a benched slot — moves the raw fraction while a real player sees nothing. Reading the tracker settled it in the worse direction: it does latch, so bench recovery was being reported outright. Regenerator is a mechanic this campaign has an audit item on for the policy's sake; it was also, quietly, a channel.

The same finding carried a train/serve consequence with no strength cost attached and a real correctness one: the serving path already wires the masked quantity, so training and serving disagreed about this channel's neighbours, and the training distribution for it was not reproducible at inference. That alone would have justified the fix.

5: a fix that reached one of two paths. The correction to the own-side HP bar — the defect §8.31 had deliberately reproduced, now fixed at the restart because the restart made it free — landed on the numpy encoder and did not reach the JAX token path that the GPU lineage actually runs. The JAX path additionally carried an indexing transposition of exactly the shape §8.31 describes: it read party slot 0's six stats where six slots' maximum HP belonged. So for a few hours the state of the world was a lineage whose encoding id declared the fix and whose arithmetic did not — the pooling-under-a-stale-identifier failure this paper has now named three times, arriving by the one route nobody had guarded, a partially applied fix. It was caught by the repository's review gate on the follow-up and closed by a port whose evidence is the encoding id itself: the manifest on the running pod digests to the third arm, which is the arm that only exists if both paths carry the fix. The three-arm versioning is what made that provable rather than assertable, and it is the mechanism §8.31 argued for, doing the job it was argued for.

The audit's negative results are reported too, because an audit that only ever finds things is not an audit. Two of its three scopes came back clean, and both were verified by execution rather than by reading. On the training targets: reward polarity has exactly one source and exactly one complement; seats are interleaved so that a row's reward, value, label and observation are all the same seat's, and the array operation that would have silently mis-paired them is the right one; the advantage estimator was cross-checked against an independently written reference recursion at three parameter settings to a maximum absolute difference of 2.4 × 10⁻⁷; truncation, bootstrap and the cross-episode boundary each behave correctly under a constructed probe. On the action space: 32 of 32 checks pass, the 89-wide space is a verified bijection, the narrow and wide spaces are a bijection on the shipped interface, all 89 head/token pairs are distinct so no two actions can share a logit, the legality mask is both built and applied in the same index space, and the exploration band points at the real switch columns rather than at the narrow-space defaults that would have addressed move slots. The class of defect that would have been unrecoverable — a systematically mis-attributed target — was the class the audit looked hardest for and did not find.

Three items were deferred with reasons rather than fixed, and the reasons are as much the record as the fixes: the unencoded volatiles and the own-side HP quantisation of §8.33, both layout- or arithmetic-shaped and therefore frozen for this lineage's life; and one genuine defect in the Showdown serving seat, which derives its history events in the narrow action space while the seat itself declares wide actions, so three of the low action ids are misread. It cannot touch the training lineage — it is evaluation-only — but it makes serving mismatched to training in exactly the channels §8.34(4) is about, and it is filed as its own change rather than folded into a correctness bundle it does not belong to.

What the ledger is for. Five defects, five mechanisms, one night: a launch gate, a launch gate again, an adversarial verifier with a proof of concept, a targeted input audit commissioned by a question, and a code-review gate on a follow-up. The protocol in §7 is often described — including in earlier revisions of this paper — as though its layers were alternative ways of catching the same thing. This night is the counterexample. Remove any one layer and one of these ships, and two of the five would have shipped as a lineage that cheats while declaring that it does not.

9Limitations and Future Work

  • Conformance is 87.4%, not 99.9%. Roughly one turn in eight still diverges from Showdown somewhere; the ranked residual (damage-roll residue 6.13%, an unclassified bucket now down to 3.30%, VOLATILE_UNMODELLED 1.19% with lockedmove at its head, duration counters) bounds how much simulator error the net can absorb, and closing it is ongoing. Damage-roll residue is now clearly the largest item, and ≈93% of it is genuine engine divergence rather than harness artifact. Two further caveats attach to the number itself: it is measured on a re-established corpus after the previous one was lost with its rented pod (§8.9), so the series has a documented discontinuity rather than a clean line; and it is an enumerate-for-scoring figure, which answers "can the engine's branch set explain what Showdown did" and not "how does the engine play."
  • A conformance number cannot certify the fixes it scores. §8.9's central finding is that a patch can move the grader and remain inert in the environment it ships to, because the grader re-supplies every turn the state the live defect corrupts. Everything this paper reports about simulator fidelity is therefore a claim about a transition-mode replay; the properties that only exist across turns are covered by behavioural tests and live readback, not by the 87.4%.
  • One reported mechanism has been withdrawn. §8.5's switch-stinginess finding does not replicate and is retracted; it had stood for three revisions. The value-calibration finding that replaces it as the headline defect is large (up to −24 points of predicted-versus-realised win rate through the middle of the range) and is healing at only +2–4 points per bucket over the run — i.e. it will not resolve on its own inside the campaign's horizon.
  • Search is not a lever at the serving budget, and possibly not at any budget we can afford. §8.10 measures the multiplier at a matched 100 ms as zero (−1.4pp, intervals overlapping), and an 18× budget as consistent with zero (+3.5pp, interval straddling the anchor) at 45 s/game. The paper's remaining hybridization hopes (below) rest on a value function that §8.5 has just shown to be badly calibrated, and should be read against that.
  • The transfer measurement carries known approximations (A3 and the open half of A13), all counted per series. A9 — the largest, opponent-action inference — is closed (§6.2); opponent slot compaction is now the biggest remaining.
  • Throughput moved from hardware into our own code, and is only half-fixed. §8.3's learner was ingest-limited at ≈3,381 rows/s by single-threaded shard decode; parallel decode (PR #404) raised it to ≈5,047 rows/s on the live box — a 1.7× real gain against a 4.5–5.6× benchmark, the difference being contention with the actors on the same machine. Whether a dedicated learner box recovers the rest is an unrun A/B, and cross-box weight staleness is still trimming ≈25% of shipped shards.
  • Two evaluation rigs disagree by ≈2.5pp, and the disagreement is now certain rather than suspected (§8.8: +2.57pp paired over 55 matched checkpoints, t = 3.70, p ≈ 0.0005). The eleven-arm estimate of +3.6pp came down with more data, but the offset stopped being explicable as luck. The headline separation from inheritance now survives on both rigs independently, so the discrepancy no longer threatens any claim — but every level in §8.8 should be read as carrying a couple of points of instrument offset on top of its stated CI, and the cause (two Foul Play builds, crash-truncation interacting with battle length, host load) is still unidentified — though §8.18 now gives the host-load branch a measured mechanism (a time-budgeted opponent plays weaker under contention, worth ≈10pp within one box), which is the first candidate the campaign has been able to name. It is a candidate, not a finding; the matched-load re-test has not run.
  • The size null is volume-bound, and the re-test never ran. §8.2 finds no advantage to 8.1M over 2.49M parameters at ≈6M games of experience on the matched-design comparison, but the (unmatched) pooled soak reads 2.8pp toward the larger model at p = 0.055. Neither is a claim about 30M or 100M games. Previous revisions said "the week run is the re-test" — it was not: the run carried a single 2.49M arm and no matched larger one, so it contains no capacity comparison. §8.12's flattening curve is therefore not evidence of a capacity ceiling, however naturally it reads as one, and §8.12 states at length why we decline that inference.
  • The week run stopped improving and we do not know why. §8.12's final third is flat on three independent cuts of the data (p = 0.23–0.30) while the same instrument resolved the earlier rise at p = 0.0002. Capacity, a saturated self-play opponent signal, the §8.5 evaluator defect, and a genuine ceiling for searchless 8 ms decisions in this format are all consistent with it, and this run separates none of them.
  • No fixed-reference abort criterion, and §8.12 is what that costs. §8.4 shows the self-play league saturates at ≈50% against a sliding frozen reference while genuine strength is still rising, so it cannot detect a plateau and cannot gate a long run. Only the external treadmill can, and it costs real battles. That is exactly how the week run ended: the flattening was visible only to 8,400 games of external evaluation against Foul Play, nothing internal to the trainer knew, and the stop was a human-directed decision read off that curve rather than a criterion the run could fire itself. A cheap fixed-reference internal opponent remains an open design problem and is now the difference between a run that can stop itself and one that cannot.
  • Every §8.4 number predates the current engine, and the week run's own physics changed mid-flight. Runs 1 and 1b trained against the 17-patch, 79.6%-conformance engine with six event-outcome channels dead; §8.8's separation is the first measurement on the corrected one, and it remains confounded with warm-start dynamics we cannot separate from the physics change itself — a step at the seam is consistent with the fidelity story and with several duller ones. The week run's training environment then changed again at iteration 4076 (§8.11), and three further times after that — a value objective at 4447, mixed precision at 5035, and a GPU serving fold (§8.13). Every one was deliberate and none was rolled back, but the consequence is blunt: the week run's curve is a four-treatment curve, and §8.12's level and flattening are properties of that composite, not of any single configuration.
  • Formats. Everything here is gen9 random battles. OU and National Dex need team pools and preview handling. Doubles now has a simulator (§8.14) with all seven build lanes merged, but no training run and no reference opponent anywhere in the world to benchmark against. Its sim2 engine has now been graded against the shipped fork on singles and lost by 25 points (§10.2), so it does not license replacing the fork; the two engines remain a doubles engine and a singles engine, and no result in this paper rests on sim2 singles fidelity. §8.19 closes ≈2pp of that distance in one day and re-ranks what is left, but the verdict is unchanged and the remaining road rests on two lanes that are in flight rather than landed. The 25-point figure is superseded by §8.20, which replaces that comparison entirely: on one instrument the gap is 1.4124pp, not 25. The verdict survives; the magnitude does not.
  • Serving now has a path and a latency problem. A checkpoint reaches the game server through a real pipeline (§10.4), default off, with three links still deliberately refusing rather than stubbed. But the first measured Rust runtime forward pass is 18.80 ms at batch 1 against §1.1's single-digit-millisecond budget and §8.2's ≈3.5 ms projection — roughly 5× over. Until that closes, the arithmetic behind "500 concurrent battles" is a projection from PyTorch timings, not from the runtime that would actually serve them.
  • The GPU simulator is still not equivalent to the simulator it was ported from, though the gap is now measured rather than open-ended. §8.15's bit-identity was proven against a commit and lapsed when the mechanics lanes landed underneath it. §8.25's union pre-grade closes most of it — 87.98% singles / 68.49% doubles against the Showdown oracle, 97.17% / 95.21% as pure transcription — but it is not 100% on either reading, and the residue is named rather than eliminated: 5,973 singles turns cost by handlers not yet ported (five whole modules, targeting the largest), 1,838 by core defects of which 79% are one structural item (turn suspension). The training lineage of §8.27 is running on that engine, so every number it produces inherits a ≈0.44pp oracle gap and a 2.83pp transcription gap against the CPU simulator this paper's fidelity work has been measuring all along.
  • The accuracy tip is measured but unmerged, and the paper reports both states. §8.24's 88.4173% is the head of an open pull request, taken on a base that carries one of §8.20's two merged fixes and not the other. The merged native bar of record is 87.7833%; the union projects to ≈88.65% if the fix sets are disjoint, which they were when measured against each other but which resolves for certain only on the merge. No conclusion in this paper rests on the difference, and the shared-instrument bar of 90.0329% is unmoved by any of it.
  • The encoder equivalence certifies sameness, not correctness — and its corpus is partly synthetic. §8.26's device encoder is bit-exact on ids, masks and legality bits and within 1 ULP on floats over 12,007 states, but 13 of 21 diversity axes were unreachable by the simulator at measurement time (weather, terrain, boosts, status all sat in handler lanes mid-merge), so ≈75% of corpus rows carry injected rather than simulated state. That is legitimate for a pure state function and is not evidence about the simulator. The limit was demonstrated concretely: two observation fields (item_known, ability_known) are wrong on this path and both encoders agree that they are wrong. And the corpus half of the suite runs on a rented pod by hand, not in CI.
  • The four-card throughput figure was arithmetic, and the arithmetic has now been measured wrong (§8.29). §8.26's ≈150–250 battles/s per card and ≈600–1,000 aggregate extrapolated one memory-capped consumer card across a device change and a memory boundary. Measured on four H100s the actors run at ≈17 battles/s per card at 1% GPU utilisation — host-bound, so the extra memory the projection was built on buys nothing. The lane that produced the measured 97.4 battles/s declined to publish the multiplication and was right to. §8.15 was the first cross-device projection this campaign got wrong; this is the second, and the standing rule that came out of the first — replace it with a measurement or retract it — is what discharged it.
  • The throughput ladder's last two rungs are unbanked. §8.29 quotes 4,884 samples/s as the furthest figure that re-derives from a committed artefact. The coordinator's log records two further rungs — shard granularity and moving evaluation off the critical path — reaching ≈4,900 with feed waits at zero, and this paper does not print them as results. Nor does it print a day-one battle total, for the same reason (§8.32 (ii)). The learner's own ceiling of ≈10,300 samples/s at a fully overlapped ingest is arithmetic off the profile, not a measurement.
  • A learner-side hang is open with its root cause retracted. The league evaluation stalled the H100 learner for >20 minutes twice, a root cause was published, a fix was applied, and the stall recurred at iteration 299 — at which point the published cause was shown to describe an evaluation configuration the run had never used. It is carried as unestablished rather than as fixed, and it bounds how much of §8.29's throughput ladder is load-bearing, since a 30-minute stall costs more than every rung on it wins back.
  • Hybridization, now with a measurement against it. The Phase-1 search machinery over a Phase-2 network was the expected configuration for compute-unconstrained battles (gym leaders), on the strength of Jaxcalibur's +100–150 Elo search increment. §8.10 measured our version of it and found nothing at the serving budget and nothing resolvable at 18× it. The ordering survives — net first, search as garnish — but the garnish is currently worth zero on our value function, and the honest next step is to fix the evaluator (§8.5) before revisiting the search.
  • The value objective aimed at §8.5's defect has no verdict. BCE was cut into the live run at iteration 4447 (§8.13) and the offline re-check returned "underpowered — no calibration verdict is warranted yet, in either direction," because the sample resolves only shifts ≥ +5 points while ordinary drift is worth +2–4. The headline defect of this paper therefore still has no measured treatment effect, only a plausible treatment.
  • Human evaluation is five games. §8.16's 2W–3L is the only post-pivot measurement against a human opponent, it is against one tester, and it is the single number in this paper carried by a session log rather than by a re-derivable artifact. It is reported for direction and cannot support a comparison to the Phase-1 1W–15L in any statistical sense.
  • Every strength percentage in this paper carries an unrecorded host term. §8.18 measures a 10-point swing on the same checkpoint against the same opponent from box load alone, because Foul Play is time-budgeted. Comparisons made within a load window are sound and that is how the 8M gate was finally read; comparisons made across windows — which includes most cross-revision comparisons in this paper, since the evaluation boxes have been shared throughout — are not cleanly attributable. We do not know how large the term is for any historical arm, because the load was never recorded alongside the result. It is recorded from now on, and the grading fleet of §8.18 removes the largest source of it going forward.
  • The 8M recipe is stable and still unproven at strength. §8.17's learning-rate fix arrests a reproducible collapse — trust region inside the healthy band, entropy drift down 3.7–5.5× — but its pre-registered strength gate failed: at iteration 260 the run sits 4.3pp below the seed it started from, with the slope positive and the level short. The honest reading is "not sample-efficient at ~1,000 games per iteration," and the alternative reading — that the recipe is merely stable-while-slowly-decaying — is not excluded by 300,000 games. The GPU-scale run is the experiment that separates them, which means the campaign's central architectural bet is currently supported by a negative result rather than by a positive one.
  • The conformance road sums, barely, and on unlanded work. §8.19's arithmetic reaches past the fork's 87.372% only by taking a 7.04pp joint ceiling whose internal overlap has never been measured, on top of two lanes that are dispatched rather than merged. This paper has already carried one ranked ledger whose top item a later re-derivation deflated by 80% (§7), and §8.19's own headline lesson is a 92× deflation of a candidate that had been filed as the biggest one left. A ledger of ceilings should be read as a ledger of ceilings. §8.20 re-prices that road on measured exposure and it no longer sums: the remaining reachable ceiling is 2.61pp with a realistic 0.9–1.9pp, against the 1.41pp the shared instrument says is owed — and the per-row ordering of the price list it replaces was wrong by up to two orders of magnitude in both directions.
  • Three revisions of engine comparison were instrument, not engine. §8.20's controlled reading is that under each engine's own grader on one paired population the fork and sim2 are indistinguishable (p = 0.70), and that the entire measured difference between them appears only once field coverage and conditioning are made identical. Every fork-versus-sim2 figure printed before it — including this paper's own −25.23pp, +0.051pp and +0.2229pp — should be read as superseded rather than refined. The lesson generalises past this comparison: two graders produce a comparison of graders, and the only fix that worked was deleting one of them, not subtracting its corrections.
  • The 1.4124pp is an upper bound, and nothing in it says how either engine plays. The residual conditioning asymmetry runs in the fork's favour (bounded ~0.2pp, lower bound on the lead ≈+1.21pp); both engines are forced to the oracle's outcome, so neither column says whether a chance point had the right probability or exists at all; 483 turns are excluded rather than explained; doubles is untouched; and efficiency — strength per millisecond at 500+ players, the actual product constraint — is unmeasured by any of it.
  • +2.12pp is a strength proxy, not ladder Elo. §8.21's separation is real and is the first one that excludes zero, but it is our own environment against our own banked checkpoint, with both nets sampling rather than playing greedily, and against a control that trained roughly twice as long. It says nothing about the human gap (1W-15L natural) or about Foul Play, which are different measurements against different opponents. Its value is as a baseline to re-run after the entropy controller, the annealer and the BCE cut have acted — and the campaign's tooling carried a −0.73pp seat bias until this measurement, so historical A-seat-only numbers from it are all depressed by about that much.
  • The +2.12pp did not hold, and the campaign's three live cuts have a negative net effect. §8.22's re-measurement reads 50.54% [50.05, 51.03] against §8.21's 52.12% [51.64, 52.61] — disjoint intervals, −1.58pp, on an instrument whose nulls reproduced run for run. It is a net effect over 700 iterations in which four things changed together and no single cut is convicted; the cuts are held rather than rolled back and the verdict waits on an iteration-5,500 re-measure. Until that lands, this paper's most-quoted training-side result is a number that its own re-run did not reproduce, and it is left in place with the re-reading beside it.
  • A falling loss is not evidence of anything in this system. Loss fell 0.7142 → 0.2864 across the same window in which strength fell, because the annealer floors the auxiliary coefficients and shrinks the objective. value_loss moved 0.5112 → 0.5142 — worse — and the opponent-action head sits at 0.5019, i.e. chance. Any curve in this paper that shows total loss should be read as a curve of a changing objective, not of a fixed one.
  • Scale bought nothing measurable at these sizes. §8.23's clean read puts the 8M at 40.17% [37.53, 42.86] (n = 1,297) against a matched 2.5M control's 41.64% under constant conditions, p = 0.34, with the h2h edge shrunk to +0.54pp — and retracts an earlier +2.96pp that did not survive newer checkpoints (now +1.10pp, p = 0.39). Combined with §8.2's null this is two independent failures to find a capacity effect at ≈6M and ≈300M samples of experience. It is not evidence that capacity does not matter; it is evidence that this campaign has never trained at a volume where it could, which is the whole argument for §8.27.
  • One of our own caveats was too pessimistic, and is corrected downward. The ≈10pp serving-load inflation this paper attributed to one evaluation box measures 1.5pp. Every cross-era comparison it qualified is healthier than stated. The correction is recorded rather than absorbed because it runs in our favour.
  • gpu-q1 is a full-information run and its strength numbers do not transfer. The reveal-mask property of §5.3 — machine-checked on the CPU environment, and the reason any of this campaign's transfer numbers mean anything — is not yet implemented on the JAX side. Both seats see the whole state. Throughput, stability and entropy control are legitimately measurable on it; win rates are not comparable to §8.4, §8.12 or §8.21, and are not ladder-transferable, until the masks land. This is disclosed at launch rather than at publication.
  • That disclosure has since been acted on, and the cost is that this paper has no GPU strength number at all. The masks landed, the full-information lineage was stopped and its results withdrawn (§6.4, §8.33), and the masked lineage is hours old. There is therefore currently no measured strength for the system this campaign is actually building — the honest position, and a worse-looking one than the position it replaced.
  • The data-parallel learner is merged and not running. §8.29 named distributing the learner as the multiplier aimed at the measured wall; §8.34 reports it surviving an adversarial FAIL → fix → PASS cycle. Its first boot on the real hardware then found a limit the two-GPU proof could not see: ≈56 GiB of model and optimizer state per rank, beside a JAX environment, does not fit two ranks into an 80 GB card. The run fell back to a single learner rank with one of four cards idle. This is a memory-envelope problem rather than a sharding one — the disjoint-claim property held — but until it is closed the learner remains the bottleneck §8.29 measured, and the fourth card is buying nothing.
  • Two observation limits are now frozen for this lineage's life, deliberately. Volatiles — Substitute, Leech Seed, confusion, Taunt, Encore, choice lock, Protect counters, Wish, the last move used — are not encoded on any channel, although the simulator publishes all of them; and our own side's HP is quantised to whole percent and rounded up, which is lossier than what a real player reads. Both are layout- or arithmetic-shaped, so changing either forces a restart, and both were declined at the one moment they were free (§8.33). They are limits of what the network can see, not bugs, and they bound what any strength number from this lineage can mean.
  • Serving and training disagree in the history channels, on the evaluation seat. The wide Showdown seat derives its history events in the narrow action space, so three low action ids are misread. It is evaluation-only and cannot reach the training lineage, but it means the standing Showdown instrument of §6.3 feeds the network a history block that is not exactly the one it trained on. Filed, not yet fixed.

10The Phase Transition: What Closed, and What Opens

This revision is a boundary. The CPU self-play era of this campaign closed with §8.12's flattening curve, and the work that follows runs on different hardware, a different simulator, and a different network. This section states what was banked, what is actually underway, and what is merely planned — kept apart, because the distinction is the whole value of the section.

10.1 What closed, and what was banked

The week run was stopped deliberately, not by a watchdog, and the shutdown was designed around a property of the training system worth recording: the replay queue is a live drain, not an archive. The learner unlinks each shard as it consumes it and a reclaimer deletes anything whose weight-lag exceeds a threshold, so at any moment the only corpus that exists is the one currently in flight. There was therefore nothing to "verify intact" — both deleters had to be stopped before anything could be preserved, in that order, and the surviving shards were then moved out of the managed queue into a path nothing scans. A system that recycles its own experience needs an explicit bank step, and discovering that at shutdown rather than before it is the kind of thing §7 exists to prevent and did not.

Banked: the final checkpoint and its exact relaunch argv (the run is resumable byte-identically from iteration 6,633 if it is ever wanted), the in-flight corpus, and three candidate teacher checkpoints with recorded hashes.

The teacher. Ranked by Wilson lower bound over all 83 completed n = 300 arms, checkpoints 4328 and 4984 tie exactly — the same 143–157 record, an LCB of 0.4208 each. The tie was broken toward 4984 on two grounds, both stated because ties broken silently are how selection bias enters: 4984 is post-BCE-cut and therefore carries the same value parameterisation as the forward direction, which makes the distillation target a match rather than a conversion; and at equal measured strength the later checkpoint has seen more data. Both were banked, so the choice is reversible at no cost.

10.2 What is actually underway

  • Distillation warm-start — finished, and its RL follow-on closed. See §8.17. The plan was to distil the banked 2.5M teacher into the ≈8M history-aware network that the GPU-scale phase will train, so that the expensive run starts warm rather than from noise. It worked: the 8.13M student reached teacher agreement 0.848 and 44.3% [38.8, 50.0] against full-strength Foul Play, statistically the teacher's equal, and is banked with checksums as the artifact of record. One finding from the corpus recon is worth carrying forward, because it made the step cheaper than expected: the existing 60-token observation already contains a 16-token history group and a 24-token matchup group (§5.1). "History-aware" is therefore substantially a change to what the encoder consumes, not a change to the data pipeline — the tokens have been produced and flattened away all along. What did not work is the CPU-rate reinforcement learning that followed, and §8.17 reports it in full: three washed runs, two falsified explanations, a misread configuration, a learning-rate root cause, a confirmation run whose trust region held for 140 iterations, and a pre-registered strength gate that failed at 39.7% against its own 44.0% seed anchor. The line is closed rather than pending. The GPU run warm-starts from the seed.
  • The launch-boundedness fix for the GPU simulator (§8.15) — measured, merged, and superseded as the bottleneck: with the kernel hardened, the binding cost moved to the host-side observation encoder, which §8.26 then moved onto the device for 44–120× on encoding and 1.73–1.84× on the full loop. The bottleneck after that is measured too, and is the _pull host copy.
  • The GPU training lineage — launched, and it is the item this whole list was for. §8.27's gpu-q1 is training: a fresh 8.19M-parameter network on the 89-wide action space, generated by the JAX environment with the device encoder, entropy-controlled from iteration zero, quarantined beside the untouched CPU fleet rather than cut over onto it. It carries the full-information banner of §8.27 until the JAX reveal masks are built, which makes it a throughput-and-stability experiment first and a strength experiment only afterwards.
  • The CPU fleet beside which it was quarantined no longer exists (§8.28). Two independent instruments closed the era on the same day: the head-to-head's third point recovered the anneal dip in full (52.31% [51.82, 52.80]) and thereby measured 2,050 iterations at +0.18 ± 0.69pp, and the run's whole 83-arm external ladder came out of the archive flat across 5,574 iterations (mean ≈42.5%, range 35.7–52.0% at n = 300). The rentals were archived, verified and destroyed by owner decision. What survives is the ruler: the 8M pinned as an immutable anchor, the fixed opponent for every GPU strength number from here.
  • The reveal mask — built, verified, and now the lineage (§8.31, §8.33). The property §5.3 rests on exists on the GPU stack, equivalence-exact against the CPU chain over 144 rows on each of two arms with zero excused cells, twelve mutants caught by name, and a distinct encoding id by construction. It was held at a seam for one day and then wired in at the cutover: the running lineage is masked, §8.27's banner no longer governs any live number, and the integration recipe the arm rejected — the shorter one, which would have shipped a leak under a masked: true manifest — is §7's positive exhibit for the round.
  • The full-information lineage — stopped, archived, and its results withdrawn (§8.33). The owner ruled the data poison and had it wiped from the live graph. Two masked successors ignited the same night, three and a half hours apart, because pre-ignition verification kept finding defects in the model's inputs and a restart is cheap only while a run is young. The live run is masked, carries the corrected HP arithmetic and the closed history-channel leak, and cost zero throughput (4,858 samples/s against 4,921). What it does not have is a strength number: the graph starts again at zero, on purpose.
  • Game-accurate evaluation — shipped, and it is a standing instrument (§8.30). The 89-wide seat plays real Showdown battles against the pinned anchor under real hidden information, on a 15-minute loop with a backfill queue. Its first ladder was monotone with a non-overlapping floor (0.00% [0.00, 3.70] untrained → 4.00% at an early checkpoint → 9.25% [6.79, 12.49] at iteration 249), which is what licenses reading a level as the lineage's rather than the seat's. Those levels are now withdrawn with the lineage that produced them (§6.4); the monotonicity result, which is about the seat, is not. The loop is unchanged and is grading the masked lineage from zero.
  • Multi-card learner training — merged, verified adversarially, and blocked on memory (§8.34). The data-parallel learner passed a FAIL → fix → PASS cycle in which the verifier falsified the safety argument the whole mechanism rested on, and it merged with the fix, a regression test for the mutant the suite could not see, and a re-checked bit-identity against the single-rank path. Its first boot on the four-card box then found a memory envelope, not a sharding problem: ≈56 GiB per rank beside a JAX environment does not fit two ranks on 80 GB. The run fell back to one learner rank; the fix is in flight; the fourth card is idle in the meantime.
  • Multi-card training — the named next multiplier, and the first one aimed at the measured bottleneck. §8.29 measured the learner as the wall: one process on one card at 3,030 samples/s against a single actor's 8,340, with 66.4% of an iteration spent ingesting shards and 62% of generation discarded stale. Two rungs took it to 4,884 (+61%) without touching the model, and the profile puts a fully overlapped ingest at ≈10,300 — still one card. Distributing the learner across the box's four cards is therefore the first multiplier that attacks the thing actually binding, and it is being designed rather than assumed: behind a flag, on disjoint file scope, with the rank-0 publish required to be byte-compatible with the single-card path so that a lineage can cross the change without its checkpoints becoming a different series. The order matters and is the lesson of this round: more actor cards were worth nothing until the consumer scales.
  • The mechanics port, at the union rather than lane by lane (§8.25) — 18.48% → 87.98% singles and 20.55% → 68.49% doubles at the five-lane union, with the residue split into 5,973 coverage turns and 1,838 core turns so that the remaining work is a named list rather than an estimate.

10.3 The road, in order

  1. Crossover — decided, and the answer was no (§10.2). sim2 stays a doubles engine; the Rust fork keeps singles. What this item becomes is the far cheaper queue the gauntlet exposed once its instrument was fixed: targeting, and the ×1.5 damage residue — and, since §8.19's re-ranking, two larger items ahead of both. The engine question re-opens only if sim2 closes the remaining distance — which since §8.20 is not the distance to 87.372%, nor to the fork's native 87.6462%, but +1.4124pp on the shared instrument, and the queue against it is re-priced: a measured ceiling of 2.61pp with a realistic 0.9–1.9pp, 77% of it in five named mechanisms, with Curse (0/346 turns agreeing) and the locked-move volatile (2/337) at the head once the queue is ranked by disagreeing turns instead of by note count. Round 7.1 builds the top two rows (§8.24): phazing and Curse together are worth +0.8694pp, 566 turns fixed against 1 broken at p = 5 × 10⁻¹²⁴, the largest single-lane accuracy move of the campaign, consuming roughly a third of the measured ceiling in one merge. The queue's new head, re-ranked by available pp rather than note count, is randomNormal targeting (+0.6186pp) and Outrage's PP accounting (+0.5524pp). The distance to the shared-instrument bar is unchanged at 90.0329%, and the engine is still not replaced.
  2. The bottleneck, which has now moved four times and is no longer on the generation side at all. §8.15's hardened kernel put 1.7–3.3× into the training path; §8.26 took the host encoder off the critical path for 1.84× at 2,048 environments; the _pull narrowing then landed at 1.54–1.75× on the step rather than the 170–250× on the pull segment that was projected (§8.32 (i)), which moved the actor's cost into the splice — 78% of the step, and its device write-back more than half of that, as 80 dispatches rather than 80 megabytes. And §8.29 then made the whole sequence secondary: on four H100s the learner consumes 3,030 samples/s against a single actor's 8,340, so 62% of generation is discarded and a fourth actor card buys disk. The question §8.26 left owing — how much batch does 80 GB buy a memory-capped loop — is answered and the answer is none, because the loop is host-bound at 1% GPU utilisation and measured ≈17 battles/s per card against a 150–250 projection. The ordered queue is therefore: distribute the learner across the four cards (the only lever aimed at the measured wall, §10.2), batch the splice's 80 uploads into one, and only then add generation. The split-fleet idea survives with its rationale inverted — H100-class silicon earns its price on the learner, and the generation side has never yet been the constraint.
  3. Randbats at GPU scale — launched 2026-09-04 as gpu-q1 (§8.27) and moved to four H100s the same night (§8.29), though not in the shape this item described: it is a fresh 89-wide lineage rather than a warm start from the distilled seed, because the observation encoding changed and the learner refuses shards across that seam. Its immediate deliverables were throughput, stability and entropy control, and it delivered all three plus one it was not asked for: §8.30's ladder, the first measurement of how much of a full-information lineage's strength survives contact with hidden information. The sizing question of §8.26 is closed and the answer was negative (item 2). That lineage no longer exists. On 2026-09-05 the owner ruled its full-information data poison; it was stopped and archived, its strength numbers withdrawn (§6.4), and replaced the same night by a masked lineage carrying the reveal mask of §8.31, the corrected own-side HP arithmetic and the closed history-channel leak of §8.34 (§8.33). The strength gate below now applies for the first time, because the masked lineage's numbers are comparable to the rest of this paper — and it has none yet.

    The strength gate stands unchanged for the masked lineage that follows it: warm-started from the distilled seed — not from any of the checkpoints trained on top of it, for the reason §8.17's gate gives — with an explicit target rather than an open-ended budget, 70% against full-strength Foul Play. §8.12's 41.5% sustained and 47.7% peak are the level to beat, and the gate is set where it is because §1.2's ladder puts ≈50% at "competitive with the best humans." The justification for the item has changed twice: it used to rest on a throughput argument, §8.17 replaced that with a strength one, and §8.22–§8.23 have now sharpened it into a starvation one — three interventions cut into a CPU-rate run produced a net −1.58pp, and 3.2× the parameters produced p = 0.34 against the external opponent. Neither result is explicable at a volume the campaign has never reached. §8.28 completes that argument and is the strongest form of it the paper has: over the CPU run's whole life, 2,050 iterations of interventions netted +0.18 ± 0.69pp against a frozen control, and 83 arms against the external opponent across 5,574 iterations were flat noise. The era did not fail at any one thing; it never moved. Its launch configuration is frozen, down to the mandatory config-override readback and the abort signature (clip_frac > 0.35, approx_kl > 0.07, or entropy above 0.75 and climbing faster than 0.001/iteration inside the first 50 iterations).

  4. Per-format specialists by distillation — National Dex and OU as students of a randbats generalist rather than as runs from scratch, which is substantially a data problem (set tables, team preview, legality) once the transfer works.
  5. Doubles, now that a doubles simulator exists (§8.14) — still with no reference opponent in the world to benchmark against, which remains the hardest measurement problem on this list.
  6. A co-evolving team builder — unchanged in ambition and still the most speculative track, but with the clearest product tie: no published system we are aware of pairs a genuine RL battler with builder co-evolution, and the pairing is what would let the AI build teams for gym leaders rather than only pilot the ones it is handed. Legality is modelled as per-target profiles, with Cobblemon datapacks as the production authority.

The plans behind items 2–4 live in the repository rather than in this paper; docs/GPU_COMPUTE_PLAN.md and docs/research/gpu-plan/ carry the detail, including the risk register whose entry R15 anticipated — correctly — that the campaign would be tempted to read a throughput measurement as a capacity argument (§8.12).

10.4 Serving, which finally has a path

§9 has carried "designed but not yet shipped" for every revision. There is now a pipeline: a checkpoint exports to ONNX plus a manifest, a pure-Rust runtime executes it inside the game server with no second native library, the 60-token observation is ported to Rust and byte-parity-tested against the Python goldens (90,312 float cells, worst divergence 5.07 × 10⁻⁷; ids, masks and legality bits exact), and the mod wires it behind a per-format config that ships off. ONNX against PyTorch over 1,000 positions moves the greedy action 0 times.

Two things about it belong in a results paper rather than a changelog. The first is a defect the work surfaced: the live observation layout had already drifted away from every trained checkpoint in existence, and nothing would have noticed — an engine patch widening the item vocabulary from 242 to 319 entries moved the served layout without a line of the schema file changing, and every checkpoint on the evaluation box declares the old width. Its item embedding table is 77 rows short of the first Z-Crystal it would ever see, and the training-time clamp would have mapped all 77 onto one wrong item. That is now a refusal with a test rather than a latent surprise, because the layout identifier folds the vocabulary digest into itself.

The second is a number that constrains the roadmap: the Rust runtime's forward pass measures 18.80 ms at batch 1, single-threaded, against 2.52 ms for the same graph in PyTorch. §1.1's budget is single-digit milliseconds on shared threads and §8.2's whole commercial argument was that a 2.49M net reaches ≈3.5 ms. The first measured serving latency is roughly five times the budget it has to meet, and closing that gap — threading, quantisation, a different runtime, or a smaller net — is now a named work item rather than an assumption. Three links in the chain are also still missing, each written as a loud refusal at its own call site rather than a silent stub, so an armed brain cannot yet play a turn and cannot pretend to.
