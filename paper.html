From Search to Self-Play: Building a Real-Time Pokémon Battle AI Under a Production Latency Budget

Status working draft Updated 2026-09-07 (round 8) Distribution private, shared at the author's discretion

Abstract

We build a Pokémon battle AI for a production Minecraft network whose binding constraint is strength per millisecond, not maximum strength: 500+ concurrent battles on shared server threads. A classical search system plateaued at ≈20% against full-strength Foul Play; a fix worth +7.7pp of offline agreement converted to only ≤+4pp of real win rate once routed through search — offline agreement only screens. We rebuilt the system as self-play PPO over a Rust simulator verified against Pokémon Showdown by differential testing (turn agreement 87.4%), training an 8.1M-parameter transformer at up to 14,462 samples/second on four H100s. A production incident — a legality-mask defect stalling 94% of actors in unwinnable battles — explains months of apparent plateau better than any hyperparameter; fixing it moved the anchor win rate from 28.15% to 39.13%. The first official Showdown-ladder appearance peaked at 1944 Elo over 90 games, against our own scripted rulers' 137-Elo read of a real 628-Elo gap. A same-day audit found the served brain blind on 44% of its trained input (an estimated +107 Elo, now fixed and staged) and Showdown-oracle agreement at 90.25%. We report the results that changed our decisions, not the path to them; a corrections table and appendix carry the rest.

1The Product Constraint, and the Pivot from Search to Self-Play

Cobblemon Eclipse runs Minecraft servers where players battle AI trainers using Cobblemon, a faithful Pokémon-mechanics implementation, with a design target of 500+ concurrent players on shared region-worker threads. This inverts the usual competitive-AI budget: Foul Play, the strongest open Pokémon engine, spends ≈100ms of a dedicated core per decision — one battle per core, a non-starter at 500 concurrent battles. Every design choice traces to maximum strength per millisecond, never maximum strength alone.

Strength is measured against a ladder of opponents adopted as each stopped predicting real performance: a baseline bot, an LLM-agent system, human ladder players, and finally full-strength Foul Play, whose defeat of skilled humans makes it the standing altimeter (≈17% ≈ 280 Elo below FP; ≈30% ≈ strong ladder play; ≈50% ≈ competitive with the best humans). Results against time-reduced Foul Play are used only for triage, never as progress, after a time-starved +4pp win once failed to survive full strength.

Phase 1 was a classical system: MCTS with determinized hidden information over a vendored Rust engine, a calibrated linear-logistic leaf evaluator (AUC 0.834), and a usage-frequency policy prior, at ≈100ms and ≈50k iterations/decision. It reached ≈20% against full-strength Foul Play and shipped as the server's strongest brain — then plateaued. Four preregistered findings located the ceiling in sequence. The search budget was not it: 100→800ms bought only ≈+1–2pp, with zero deadline hits. Labels were not it: distilling 1.11M Foul Play teacher decisions into the linear prior moved teacher agreement +0.56pp against a +10pp bar. Capacity was it: holding labels and inputs fixed and changing only the function class — a set-conditional network scoring each candidate against the others — was worth +7.14pp top-1 teacher agreement (+7.70pp paired, [+7.24, +8.16], 129,428 held-out decisions; a 2×2 ablation showed the gain needs capacity and set context jointly). And offline agreement was shown to be only a screening metric: routed through search as a root prior, the +7.7pp converted to ≤+4pp of win rate (best estimate +1.8). We adopted a standing rule from it — preregister behavioural endpoints against a real opponent; offline agreement only screens — that governs every accuracy number in this paper (§3).

Combined with the public Jaxcalibur result (a pure-JAX self-play recipe reaching top-human-level gen9 randbats play at ≈100M games/week on one H100, with search adding only +100–150 Elo on top of a trained policy), the conclusion was that strength lives in capacity × experience volume, and our 3,000-game corpora were four orders of magnitude short. The pivot was made 2026-08-30. Phase 2 (§2) rebuilds the system from scratch as self-play reinforcement learning at that scale, over the same verified simulator, with a transfer-measurement harness (§3) that runs during training rather than only at the end. Everything after this section is Phase 2.

2The System

Architecture. A 60-token observation per decision — 1 field, 2 active, 5 candidate-move, 12 team, 16 recent-event history, and 24 injected matchup tokens — over categorical id slots (species, item, ability, move, types, tera, status) and 269 float channels. A pre-norm transformer, 8.1M parameters (256/1024/8) with a 2.49M (128/512/8) variant, four heads: policy (14 actions), value, and two auxiliary heads (opponent-next-action, opponent-set). Training is PPO, undiscounted 1/0 reward, entropy and KL-to-uniform regularisation, following the Jaxcalibur recipe.

The simulator, and how we know it tells the truth. Training runs over the same vendored Rust engine Phase 1 searched, which raises the central question of any home-grown simulator: is the physics right? The answer is continuous differential testing against the official Showdown implementation, pinned as the oracle — a dumper emits per-turn fixtures from real random battles, a comparator drives the Rust engine through the same battles, and every divergence is classified by mechanic into a named, sized, status-tracked registry. Engine corrections land as a numbered, hash-guarded patch ledger (28 numbered, 25 landed). Turn agreement rose from a shipped 31.1% through successive waves to 87.372% on a 980-battle, 65,321-turn corpus — the canonical reference from that point forward, because it is the only one whose corpus anyone can still open. A conformance number answers "can the engine's branch set explain what Showdown did" (a mean of ≈57 branches per turn), not "what does one played-out game look like," which the training environment answers separately by sampling one roll per turn. Two of five originally-open divergence classes turned out to be defects in the measurement harness, not the engine — a conformance number is only as good as its comparator.

Hidden information, machine-checked. A self-play agent that trains on information a real opponent would hide posts beautiful curves that do not transfer. A reveal tracker publishes eleven mask arrays of what each seat has legitimately observed, and the encoder consumes only the masked view; the property is enforced by tests — two battles differing in exactly one hidden fact must produce byte-identical viewer tokens and differing owner tokens, Python and Rust twins of the same argument. Three real leaks were found and closed this way during construction.

Throughput and cost. The full-fidelity environment runs at ≈327k transitions/second on the sims box. On one H100, the full loop sustained 4,551 samples/second at 8.1M parameters and 5,551 at 2.49M — 238k and 289k games per GPU-hour, at $14.13 per million self-play games in the first 24 hours. That figure changed by two orders of magnitude once the loop moved to dedicated hardware and the real bottleneck was found — §4 reports what it was, twice.

3Measuring Honestly

Self-play curves cannot certify strength — a network can master its own simulator's quirks. This section is what a reader cannot reconstruct from the code: the instruments themselves, and why each exists.

The adapter. A transfer harness plays training checkpoints in real Showdown battles against Foul Play through poke-env, thinking ≈8ms on CPU with no search. The design rule: it builds environment-contract buffers and then calls the same code objects the trainer uses, so the only place training and serving can diverge is one translation file — exactly where testing concentrates. A second, 89-wide adapter for the GPU lineage was built as five new files that touch not one line of any existing one, because the shared files it reuses are precisely the files whose tests certify the training/serving seam.

The golden, and the approximations ledger. A cross-path golden encodes 200 positions through both paths and compares 3.37M values; the tolerated divergence is 70 values (0.0021%), all on one explained channel, pinned by a mutation-tested assertion. Everything the adapter approximates is a numbered item (A1–A13) in a public ledger with an expected cost and a measurement plan; the two capable of moving results are tracked in every series report. The largest, inferred opponent actions (unobserved on ≈55% of decisions), was closed by reading Showdown's own protocol line instead of inferring one — the unknown-action rate fell 56.4% → 19.8%.

Two accuracy instruments, named apart, because conflating them has cost this campaign real time. Showdown agreement (grade.py, rollsweep.py) compares the CPU simulator, sim2, against the Showdown oracle — this is "accuracy." Port parity (train_parity_gate.py) compares the GPU port, jaxport, against sim2 — this is an equality, not an accuracy claim; the gate's own docstring states it in two lines: "sim2 is graded against Showdown. The GPU port is graded against sim2." A high parity number says the port reproduces the CPU simulator; it says nothing about the CPU simulator's own distance from real Pokémon. This paper uses "accuracy" to mean the first comparison only, and flags every place a parity figure could be mistaken for it (§6).

The one-instrument, one-field-set, one-population rule. The clearest resolved training-side separation this campaign produced (§4) came only after collapsing two disagreeing internal comparators onto one comparator, one conditioning rule, and one paired population — at which point a previously-reported +3.98pp gap between two supposedly-identical instruments fell to +0.06pp, and the real signal, +1.4pp at p = 7.6×10⁻²², became visible underneath it. The rule generalises: a strength or accuracy number is not comparable across two reports unless the field set, the conditioning, and the population are all pinned to be the same thing twice.

The withdrawn-numbers ledger. Four numbers were withdrawn by our own verification rather than by an outside challenge, and we report them as method rather than erase them. An early 5% series was measured through an adapter dropping ≈110 "special" moves from the agent's own moveset. A conformance headline was measured with a driver predating one of the five fixes it reported. A switch-stinginess finding survived three revisions before a mismatched-denominator error was caught — the agent switches 2.6× more than the opponent, not less. And every strength number the full-information GPU lineage ever produced — including its best-ever reading, 18.67% [13.24, 25.66] against the pinned anchor — was retired whole not because the arithmetic was wrong but because the network had trained on the opponent's exact stats, item, moves and HP; the instrument that produced those numbers survives the retirement, the levels do not.

The human-tracking ruler, and why Foul Play is primary. Every internal strength number is against an opponent we chose. Two findings this year say that matters. First, a suite of five scripted archetypes plus two controls, built to discriminate a 1384-Elo ladder checkpoint from a 2012-Elo one, separates them by only +90 to +137 Elo-equivalent depending on rung — against a real gap of +628 Elo on the official ladder. Every scripted rung we own sits at 82–94% against our best net; a ruler that saturates cannot resolve, and one that compresses 628 Elo of real separation into 137 will report "the lineage improved" long before the ladder agrees. Second, against Foul Play, our best net wins only 39–42% — the anchor scores 39.23% [35.40, 43.20] at n=599 — and that number has never come near saturating in either direction across the campaign. Foul Play is therefore promoted from a demoted milestone gate back to the primary human-tracking ruler: it is the only opponent we own with real headroom left in both directions, and the only one anywhere near human ladder strength.

4Results That Changed Decisions

4.1 The size ablation: the verdict is parity

A 2.49M-parameter net (¼ the parameters of the 8.1M arm) trained on an identical environment, budget and hyperparameters. Three matched comparisons all read null: whole-run pooled 23.2% vs 23.8% (p = 0.54), late-run pooled 27.3% vs 27.2% (p = 0.96), best single arm 36.0% vs 34.0% (p = 0.77). At n = 3,500/arm the test resolves 3pp; it saw 0.6pp against the larger model. Eight 500-game soaks confirm it best-against-best (29.0% vs 27.4%, p = 0.57), with an honest wrinkle: pooling all soaked arms leans 2.8pp toward the larger model at p = 0.055, from an unmatched checkpoint-selection set. Decision: buy games, not parameters — the quarter-size net generates 22% more experience per GPU-hour and became the configuration scaled to the week run. Caveat kept live: this is a null at one experience volume (≈6M games); it was never re-tested at 5× the volume as originally planned.

4.2 The actor, profiled: 0.19% of the work was the game

Before spending on distributed training, thirty instrumented actor rollouts found shard packing (58.3% of wall) and a device→host→device observation round trip (31.8%) dominating actor time. Inside the 3.7% spent on simulation, the JAX battle kernel itself is 0.19% of actor wall — outweighed 11.7:1 by the host-side team draw in the auto-reset splice. Two independent instruments agree: live actors run at 7.0–8.8% SM utilisation on exactly one CPU core each. Decision: throughput work was redirected from the simulator kernel (already re-priced to 0.32% of wall) to packaging and the device-round-trip, via a zero-copy observation path verified bit-identical on 19 of 19 columns; n_envs scaling was abandoned past its already-reached knee (2,048→4,096 bought only 1.05×).

4.3 The learner as the wall, and why eight H100s is 2 learners + 6 actors

On four H100s, one learner card ran 3,030 samples/s against a single actor's 8,340 — actors over-fed the learner eightfold, and 62% of generation was discarded stale. Profiling put 66.4% of an iteration in shard ingest (a fifth of that a checksum) against 28.8% in the actual gradient step. Two rungs, corrected as measured rather than as claimed (a minibatch change A/B'd at +43% settled at +20% live; disabling checksums over-delivered at +35.7%), took the loop to 4,884 samples/s. Software work alone later reached 14,462 samples/s (3.24× in about a day, no hardware bought): bf16 + compilation + an index-cache fix + a direct-read ingest fix, with a tf32-not-fp32 premise correction that means every earlier "would have flattered every arm by 60%." Decision: measured on 2026-09-06, actors generate 13,741 rows/s against a learner eating ≈7,600/s — 1.8 actor-cards of surplus per learner card — so an eight-card box only helps as 2 learners + 6 actors, and the owner's proposed 5-learner/3-actor shape is inverted (it starves). Standing rule: no eight-card box unless it is at most 2× today's cost and measurably delivers ≈2× the speed.

4.4 The livelock: a simulator that cannot end a battle is a curriculum

The GPU lineage was flat since ≈iteration 9,000; throughput read healthy throughout because the rows kept flowing. Instrumentation found 94% of every actor's 2,048 environment slots parked in a battle that could never end, outcome_valid at ≈2%, and ≈5,500 rows per game against a healthy ~80. A uniform-random policy in the same environment stranded 0 of 64 slots; the trained policy stranded 62 of 64 — the environment was only broken for a policy that had learned something. Root cause: the legality mask gated moves and Tera on "asked" rather than "asked and alive," so a forced replacement lit a fainted Pokémon's full-PP moveset; both engine trees carried the defect identically, so the two-tree equivalence gate agreed on the wrong answer. Decision: the fleet was paused at iteration 14,289 and relaunched on the fixed tree. Measured before/after: games/iter 3.9 → 415, rows/game 5,500 → 79, acting fraction 0.57 → 0.91, and the anchor curve 28.15% [26.64, 29.71] → 39.13% [37.84, 40.44] against a pre-fix peak of 33.58%. The lineage was not plateauing; it was being trained downhill by an environment that could not end a battle.

4.5 The entropy controller was a limit cycle

The entropy-bonus dual-ascent controller drove its coefficient roughly fifteen-fold up and 98% back down, collapsing resolved-outcome rows 0.108 → 0.004 (27×). This was not one event: cross-correlating entropy against log-coefficient gave near-zero correlation at zero lag and a peak at lag ≈245 against an ≈880-iteration period — quadrature, the signature of a loop sitting at unity gain (0.05 × 0.133 × 878 ÷ 2π = 0.93). No existing guard could see it: the convergence suite's synthetic plant, a single first-order lag, is unconditionally stable at any gain, so it could not exhibit the failure by construction. Decision: an anti-windup guard (hold-on-prediction plus a travel budget) shipped, cutting peak-to-peak swing 1.69→0.60 nats without abolishing the cycle — it bounds rather than removes the residual gain — and the step rate was separately cut to a margin the describing-function analysis priced (0.015), after which entropy settled and stopped hunting. Measured strength cost across pre/excursion/post windows (25.63% / 24.65% / 24.57%) sits inside checkpoint-to-checkpoint scatter: no dent is visible, not "provably zero."

4.6 The reveal mask, built and verified before it shipped

The Rust reveal tracker (§2) was ported to the GPU stack as a pure function over two state pytrees, applied to the projection rather than the finished tensor — masking derived channels (damage chains, matchup grids) requires masking inputs, not outputs, or a leak with no symptom survives in the channels a policy leans on hardest. Against the production masked chain: 144 rows on each of a tracked-planes and a random-planes arm, zero mismatches, zero excused cells, on three independent stacks; twelve of thirteen injected mutants caught, the thirteenth reported as a finding (a PP gate with no channel to protect) rather than patched away. Decision: the arm was held at a clean seam rather than force-integrated, because the shorter wiring path leaked (masked tokens beside unmasked history under a masked: true manifest — worse than an unmasked run because the unmasked run is at least labelled honestly); it shipped at the next lineage restart, where a live indexing defect it had deliberately reproduced was fixed for free.

4.7 The ladder debut, and the 628-Elo gap our own rulers cannot see

On 2026-09-05 the frozen 8M anchor played rated gen9randombattle on the official Showdown ladder — the first outside-world number in this campaign's history. Day one: 1000 → a peak of 1944 Elo, 65–25 over 90 games in ≈7 hours, zero fallback moves, every battle a public replay. Separately, ladder-calibrated ratings for two checkpoints (anchor ≈2012, a wide net ≈1384) put the real gap at +628 Elo; our best scripted opponent (abyssal, properly powered) reads only +137 Elo-equivalent between the same two checkpoints, and every archetype rung under-reads the human gap by 4.6–8.6×. A companion finding corrects an earlier claim in the other direction: an n=400 read that called the private anchor eval "blind" to the same two checkpoints was itself underpowered, not a property of the instrument — at n=600 the same eval separates them 90.3% vs 84.0%, disjoint, +100 Elo-equivalent. Decision: discrimination is not calibration, and our internal rulers are correctly ordered but badly scaled; Foul Play (§3) is promoted to primary specifically because it is the only rung with headroom left to close a gap this size.

4.8 The search multiplier: a measured zero, and a screening lesson

Wrapping the trained network in a determinized one-ply PUCT search at its own 100ms serving budget moved win rate by −1.4pp against the same checkpoint and opponent (38.7% greedy vs 37.3% searched, overlapping intervals) — a 100ms budget buys ≈37 simulations, too few to outvote a confident policy prior, and the search agreed with greedy on 97.3% of decisions. An 18× unmatched budget made the search act on 13.9% of decisions rather than 2.7%, but still did not clear the anchor's interval (+3.5pp, still straddling). This is the same lesson Phase 1 (§1) taught from the other side: the earlier +7.7pp of offline function-class improvement converted to only ≤+4pp of win rate once routed through a real search and a real opponent. Decision: no more search-parameter tuning until the simulation budget moves an order of magnitude; improving the value function dominates improving the search; and an earlier claim that cheaper GPU inference was the enabling condition for search strength is explicitly retired — the one measurement of that conversion is consistent with zero.

4.9 TODAY — the served brain was blind on 44% of its input

Tokens 20–35 of the 60-token observation are a 16-step, 68-channel history block, populated on every training step since the encoder was written — and zeroed on every live decision the Minecraft server has ever made (live.rs sets history_len = 0 by deliberate parity choice). Measured: a history-trained net serving that block zeroed scores 0.6489 [0.6368, 0.6610] (n = 6,000) against itself fed, and the gap grows with training — ≈+107 Elo. A companion result shows the tokens carry a dependency, not skill — a net trained --no-history from scratch learns identically well (flat null, 700 iterations) and the two nets, each served in its own condition, tie (0.5073 [0.4947, 0.5200]). Decision: both are true and compose rather than conflict — train the next lineage without history, and feed history at serve time to the checkpoints that already exist (fixed in Rust, PR #638, 0ms measured inference cost). Boot-tested and staged to test only; prod/arena/lunar were explicitly not touched, because a coordinator message claiming authority to widen the deploy scope is not SPG's own word, and only SPG can lift that restriction.

4.10 TODAY — the 8M seed-wash, and the launch recipe that fixes it

A warm-started lineage (anchor-seed-v1.pt, itself measured at 49.58% [46.76, 52.41] against the anchor it was seeded from — a dead draw, exactly as predicted) diverged to 41.05% [37.80, 44.38] 599 iterations later, reproducing a standing "plateau at 41%" reading to within a point. The learner's own metrics named the cause exactly: iteration 0 logged approx_kl = 0.747 nats against a policy carrying 0.836 nats of entropy — one PPO update rewrote nearly the entire policy, because the seed carries no optimiser moments and the restart ran with no LR warm-up. A separate, real train/serve skew was found and fixed on the way (an 89-wide action id lifted incorrectly into a 14-wide history ring) but moved nothing significant (−3.2pp and +0.2pp on the two checkpoints, neither significant) — it was not the explanation. Decision: relaunch from the same proven-good seed with --anchor-lr-warmup, a decaying --anchor-kl-coef anchored to the frozen seed, and the shard drain no longer treated as optional; the gate is approx_kl at iteration 0 landing in the same 0.03–0.08 band as every later iteration, with the first ruler point expected at 48–52%, not 40%.

4.11 TODAY — argmax is right for the served net

Two independent lanes had found gpu-m2 gains ≈+9.5pp by sampling its policy instead of taking the argmax. Measured directly on the network the server actually serves — the frozen 8M anchor, against Foul Play, mirrored, 600 games/arm — sampling costs 10.45pp: 42.17% argmax vs 31.72% sampled, disjoint intervals, p = 0.0002, ≈−78 Elo, and every temperature in between is worse than argmax too. Decision: ship nothing; the server's greedy, always-argmax decode (no temperature, no top-p, no RNG) is already the right one for the checkpoint it serves. The earlier +9.5pp is real about gpu-m2 against one fixed argmax opponent and evaporates (−1.67pp, p = 0.53) the moment the opponent changes — a fixed-opponent artefact, not a property of decoding.

4.12 TODAY — the outcome-supervised value term buys the endgame and sells the mid-game

A dose-response test of --value-outcome-coef (0, 0.1, 0.25) on a held-out shard split shows it does exactly what it targets: outcome-anchored calibration error falls 0.0306 → 0.0059 → 0.0040. But Brier score at 21–60 decisions from the end — the band a one-ply search would evaluate its leaves in — gets worse at every dose (0.2143 → 0.2183 → 0.2228, against a measured noise floor of 0.0029), and full-battle calibration also worsens overall (probe ECE 0.0079 → 0.0205 → 0.0326). No dose moved measured strength (every arm within noise of 0.500, n=600). Decision: no dose ships; the term is refused by its own pre-registered gate (0.1 already costs 21–60 Brier; 0.25 crosses the calibration tripwire). What the run needs is still the bootstrap horizon reaching into the mid-game, which is a separate, already-running intervention.

4.13 TODAY — DDP tuning: never raise the learning rate

A controlled sweep on the live learner's real batch shape, gated on strength (700 mirrored games per arm against both a control and the shared seed) found exactly one arm that moves anything: --learning-rate 6e-4 is 6.07pp weaker than its own seed (43.93% [40.37, 47.70]), agreeing with two independent dynamics signals (value_loss +4.5%, clip_frac 0.226). Every other arm — a lower LR, two minibatch sizes — reads null against both the control and the seed; the control itself scores 50.79% against the checkpoint it started from, meaning 60 iterations (~0.007% of the campaign) produces no strength change this instrument can resolve at all, which bounds how much weight the null arms can bear. --minibatch-size 16384 halves both approx_kl and clip_frac at zero throughput or strength cost, held in reserve as the cheapest stabiliser available if the DDP-era KL rise is ever judged a problem. Decision: leave LEARNER_EXTRA alone; the one lever worth banning is banned.

4.14 TODAY — the ladder autopsy: no blunder class the anchor lacks

A comparative autopsy of the wide net against the pinned anchor found no missing tactical category — no blunder class the anchor avoids and the challenger commits. What separates them instead is calibration: the challenger's critic is measurably over-confident relative to the anchor's (Brier 0.2417 vs 0.2048) and switches 28% more often than the anchor under matched conditions. The critic's own horizon is implicated but not yet resolved — a separate, in-progress lane (critic-horizon.md) found that the learner's --rollout-steps flag does not set the bootstrap horizon in a fed run at all (the actor's own compute_gae call site does that, and a shard's advantages arrive pre-computed), which changes what any horizon experiment run so far can be read to have tested. Decision: stop attributing the gap to a missing skill and start treating it as an over-confidence problem in the value function that a search (§4.8) cannot fix if the value function underneath it is the thing that is wrong.

4.15 TODAY — Showdown agreement 89.26 → 90.25% across three waves

Three consecutive waves against the same corpus (984 fixtures, 64,985 graded records) moved roll-conditioned Showdown agreement 89.2637% → 90.0946% → 90.2470%, fixing a Ditto transform/revert mechanic that was entirely missing (Imposter, Transform, and the two-seat clearVolatile revert) and two Showdown refusal cases the simulator had not modelled. Of the remaining gap, 86.7% of all HP-mismatch error is not a residual-fraction bug at all — it is plain damage magnitude, smeared across 204 abilities with no single attributable cause above 5%; only two of the residual buckets (Harvest, a 16-sample Soundproof coincidence) are real identifications. Decision: stop chasing named residual-fraction classes as the next accuracy item — the census that would have ranked them third is itself the finding that they are noise — and treat the remaining ≈10% as a damage-formula problem, not an enumerable defect list.

5What We Know We Do Not Know

The sim gap, as an open ceiling question. Two very different starting points — a run trained from scratch for thousands of iterations, and a lineage warm-started from a checkpoint already at 49.58% against the anchor — both converge to ≈41% before the seed-wash defect (§4.10) was diagnosed. That coincidence is not yet explained. It may be a statement about the training signal, about the environment, or about a real ceiling this simulator's fidelity imposes; the corrected relaunch (§4.10) is the experiment that can tell them apart, and it has not yet reported.

The critic's horizon. critic-horizon.md is an open lane, not a closed result. What is established: the value-shadow term over-fits the endgame at the mid-game's expense (§4.12), the challenger's critic is measurably over-confident relative to the anchor's (§4.14), and the learner-side --rollout-steps flag does not touch the bootstrap horizon in a fed run at all — only the actor's own GAE call site does, and there is currently no command-line path to gamma < 1 either. What is not established is whether lengthening the actor's real horizon fixes the over-confidence, makes no difference, or interacts with the value-outcome term in some new way.

Loss composition. By raw loss share, 94.5% of the 8M's training loss is the two supervised auxiliary heads (aux_opp 73.5%, aux_set 21.1%) against a PPO policy surrogate at 0.63% — the reading that once supported "the policy objective is a rounding error." A direct gradient-share measurement on the shared trunk corrects the inference without correcting the arithmetic: the policy surrogate holds 26.8% of the trunk's gradient against the opponent-action head's 42.3% — closer by an order of magnitude than the loss share suggests, because a normalised PPO surrogate sits near zero in value by construction while its derivative does not. A loss share is not a gradient share, and which one governs what a shared trunk actually learns is not fully settled.
